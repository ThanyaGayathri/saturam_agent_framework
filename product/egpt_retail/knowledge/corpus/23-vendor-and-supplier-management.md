## Executive Summary

Vendor and supplier management is the structured discipline by which retailers govern the selection, performance, contractual terms, risk exposure, and strategic alignment of their supply base. It spans transactional activities—routing guides, chargeback enforcement, payment terms—and strategic initiatives such as Joint Business Planning (JBP), Vendor Managed Inventory (VMI), and ethical sourcing programs. For a retailer generating $100B in annual sales, even a 0.5% improvement in supplier fill rates can recover tens of millions in lost sales, while aggressive trade terms negotiation directly expands gross margin by 150–300 basis points (McKinsey, 2023). The discipline has evolved from adversarial price extraction to collaborative value creation, with Walmart and P&G's 1987 VMI experiment widely cited as the turning point that demonstrated mutual benefit in data-sharing and joint planning. Retailers that treat supplier management as a strategic capability—not a back-office function—outperform peers on both in-stock rates and category profitability.

**Why this matters**: Poor supplier management costs U.S. retailers an estimated $1 trillion annually in lost sales from out-of-stocks alone (IHL Group, 2023), and non-compliance chargebacks, trade term disputes, and ethical sourcing failures represent additional material risks that directly erode operating margins.

## 1. Vendor Management Framework

### 1.1 The Four-Stage Vendor Lifecycle

**Vendor Selection**
- **Definition**: The formal process by which a retailer evaluates and approves new suppliers against predefined capability, quality, financial, and compliance criteria before issuing a purchase order.
- **Mechanics**: Retailers issue a Request for Proposal (RFP) or Request for Quotation (RFQ) that specifies product specifications, volume expectations, compliance requirements (data exchange capability, insurance, certifications), and pricing targets. Qualified vendors complete a Supplier Qualification Form covering financial stability, manufacturing capacity, quality management systems (e.g., ISO 9001, BRC), and ethical sourcing certifications. A cross-functional selection team—typically involving merchant, supply chain, legal, and finance—scores responses against a weighted rubric before awarding business.
- **Example**: Target's "Supplier Qualification Assessment" requires all new food vendors to hold a GFSI-recognized food safety certification (SQF, BRC, or FSSC 22000) and demonstrate purchase order, invoice, and shipment notice transaction capability before the first purchase order is issued. Target's sourcing team also conducts factory audits on new private-label apparel suppliers before approval, using a third-party auditor such as Bureau Veritas or Intertek (Target Supplier Standards, 2024).
- **Business Implication**: A retailer that bypasses a formal qualification gate—accepting a supplier without food safety certification or data exchange readiness—absorbs that supplier's compliance deficiency through distribution center exceptions, chargebacks, and potential product recalls; the selection gate is where supply chain risk is either blocked at the door or transferred inward permanently.

**Vendor Onboarding**
- **Definition**: The structured integration process that transitions an approved vendor from contract execution to operational trading, including system setup, routing guide acknowledgement, and first-order fulfillment.
- **Mechanics**: Onboarding typically takes 60–90 days for a new domestic vendor and 90–180 days for an import vendor. Key activities include: purchase order, invoice, and inventory data exchange testing; item setup in the retailer's Product Information Management (PIM) system; UPC/barcode validation; warehouse routing guide acknowledgement; insurance certificate submission; and compliance training. Most large retailers assign a dedicated Vendor Relations Coordinator to shepherd new suppliers through this process.
- **Example**: Walmart's Supplier Center portal onboards approximately 100,000 active suppliers globally. New suppliers must complete the Walmart Standards for Suppliers training module and pass data exchange certification before any purchase order is generated. Failure to meet readiness requirements within 30 days of contract execution triggers a hold on purchase order issuance.
- **Business Implication**: Merchants who treat onboarding as an IT task rather than a commercial milestone routinely lose 60–120 days of sales velocity on new products because suppliers are not operationally ready at launch; tying first purchase order issuance to onboarding milestone completion closes this gap.

**Vendor Performance Management**
- **Definition**: The ongoing measurement, review, and improvement cycle applied to active suppliers using scorecards, structured business reviews, and escalation protocols.
- **Mechanics**: Performance is tracked monthly against KPIs (fill rate, on-time delivery, defect rate, compliance score). Quarterly Business Reviews (QBRs) convene merchant, supply chain, and supplier teams to review scorecard results, address root causes of misses, and agree corrective action plans. Vendors falling below threshold scores (typically below 90% on fill rate or below 95% on OTIF) are placed on a Performance Improvement Plan (PIP) with 60–90 days to remediate.
- **Example**: Home Depot's "Supplier Performance Dashboard" scores all vendors on a 1,000-point scale across quality, service, and compliance. Vendors scoring below 700 points are placed on a formal PIP; those below 500 for two consecutive quarters are reviewed for delisting. Approximately 3–5% of Home Depot's active vendor base is on PIP at any given time (Home Depot Supplier Relations, 2023).
- **Business Implication**: A buyer who does not conduct QBRs with scorecard data in hand has no factual leverage in annual trade terms negotiations; the scorecard is the evidential basis for demanding operational improvement from underperforming vendors or rewarding high-performers with incremental distribution.

**Vendor Offboarding**
- **Definition**: The controlled process of terminating a supplier relationship, including final settlements, inventory liquidation, knowledge transfer, and replacement sourcing.
- **Mechanics**: Offboarding is triggered by strategic discontinuation (category exit, private label substitution), performance failure, compliance breach, or financial instability. The retailer issues a termination notice per the contract's notice period (typically 30–90 days), executes a final inventory buy-in or return, settles any outstanding chargebacks or deductions, and conducts a knowledge transfer if the supplier held exclusive product specifications or tooling.
- **Example**: When Sears Holdings delisted approximately 300 mid-tier apparel vendors in 2018 during its bankruptcy restructuring, the absence of formal offboarding protocols created $180M in disputed deductions and unresolved inventory positions, highlighting the financial consequence of unmanaged vendor exit (Retail Dive, 2018).
- **Business Implication**: Category managers who begin offboarding planning only after deciding to delist a supplier consistently face 90–120 days of supply disruption while the replacement vendor is onboarded; maintaining a "shadow qualification" process for credible alternatives to all at-risk suppliers is the discipline that prevents this gap.

> **Key Takeaway**: The vendor lifecycle is a capital-allocation decision, not just an administrative process. Each stage—from selection criteria to offboarding protocol—determines where gross margin dollars are created or destroyed.

## 2. Vendor Scorecards

### 2.1 KPI Framework and Scoring Methodology

**Vendor Scorecard** *(also: Supplier Performance Scorecard, Vendor Report Card)*
- **Definition**: A structured measurement tool that quantifies a supplier's operational performance across multiple dimensions—typically fill rate, on-time delivery, quality, and compliance—on a regular (monthly or quarterly) cadence.
- **Mechanics**: Scorecards assign weighted scores across KPI categories, producing a composite score that drives relationship tiering and commercial consequences. Weights reflect strategic priority: for a grocery retailer, fill rate and on-time delivery might carry 70% of the composite score, while for a fashion retailer, quality and compliance dominate. Scorecards are shared with suppliers in advance so they understand the measurement criteria and can build their own internal tracking.
- **Example**: Kroger's "Supplier Scorecard" weights fill rate at 40%, on-time delivery at 30%, invoice accuracy at 20%, and sustainability compliance at 10%. Suppliers achieving ≥ 95% composite are designated "Preferred" and receive priority shelf placement and promotional co-investment; those below 80% face chargeback escalation and reduced future purchase order volumes (Kroger Supplier Portal, 2023).
- **Business Implication**: A merchant uses scorecard tier classifications to drive two concrete commercial actions: rewarding Preferred vendors with incremental shelf space or new product launch eligibility, and issuing chargebacks or volume reductions to Probation vendors—making the scorecard a margin-building tool rather than a reporting courtesy.

**Sample Vendor Scorecard Template — KPIs and Scoring**

| KPI | Definition | Measurement Method | Weight | Target | Excellent | Acceptable | At Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fill Rate** | Units shipped ÷ Units ordered | Shipment notice vs. purchase order comparison | 35% | ≥ 98% | ≥ 98% | 94–97.9% | < 94% |
| **On-Time Delivery (OTD)** | Shipments arriving within ±1 day of PO must-arrive-by date | DC receipt scan vs. MABD on purchase order | 25% | ≥ 97% | ≥ 97% | 93–96.9% | < 93% |
| **OTIF (On Time In Full)** | Composite of on-time + fill rate at line level | DC confirmation against purchase order | 20% | ≥ 98.5% | ≥ 98.5% | 95–98.4% | < 95% |
| **Defect Rate / AQL** | Units rejected at DC ÷ Total units received | DC quality inspection | 10% | ≤ 0.5% | ≤ 0.5% | 0.6–1.5% | > 1.5% |
| **Invoice Accuracy** | Invoices matching PO price/quantity without dispute | Accounts payable match rate | 5% | ≥ 99% | ≥ 99% | 96–98.9% | < 96% |
| **Compliance Score** | Routing guide adherence, label accuracy, packaging compliance | DC audit + chargeback log | 5% | ≥ 98% | ≥ 98% | 94–97.9% | < 94% |

**Composite Score Calculation**:

```
Composite Score = Σ (KPI Score × Weight)

Example:
Fill Rate:        96% × 0.35 = 33.6
OTD:              94% × 0.25 = 23.5
OTIF:             95% × 0.20 = 19.0
Defect Rate:      99% × 0.10 =  9.9
Invoice Accuracy: 98% × 0.05 =  4.9
Compliance:       97% × 0.05 =  4.85

Composite Score = 95.75 → "Acceptable" tier
```

**Supplier Tier Classification**:

| Tier | Composite Score | Label | Commercial Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tier 1 — Strategic | ≥ 96% | Preferred/Elite | JBP eligibility, priority shelf, co-investment |
| Tier 2 — Approved | 88–95.9% | Approved | Standard trading terms, annual review |
| Tier 3 — Conditional | 78–87.9% | Probation | Performance Improvement Plan, reduced POs |
| Tier 4 — At Risk | < 78% | At Risk | Escalation, delisting review |

> **Key Takeaway**: A vendor scorecard's value is not in the measurement—it is in the commercial consequence attached to each tier. Scorecards without consequences become courtesy reports; scorecards tied to shelf space and future volume drive supplier behavior.

## 3. Trade Terms Negotiation

### 3.1 Trade Terms Mechanics and Glossary

**Trade Terms** *(also: Commercial Terms, Supplier Terms)*
- **Definition**: The contractual financial arrangements between a retailer and supplier that govern the total cost of goods, including invoice price, volume rebates, promotional allowances, and payment timing.
- **Mechanics**: Trade terms are typically negotiated annually as part of the buying cycle, often in conjunction with the Annual Operating Plan (AOP). The retailer's merchant and the supplier's key account manager negotiate a "total terms package" that is more important than invoice price alone—a 2% retrospective rebate on $50M in annual sales generates $1M in margin that a pure price negotiation would not capture. The total terms package is documented in a signed commercial agreement and governs every financial transaction between the two parties for the fiscal year.
- **Example**: Tesco's buying team negotiates a "total cost of ownership" trade terms package with each major supplier that can include up to 12 separate line items of allowances, rebates, and contributions. Tesco's infamous 2014 accounting scandal, which overstated profits by £263M, was partly driven by the premature recognition of trade term income before the associated sales volumes were achieved (BBC, 2014).
- **Business Implication**: A buyer who manages only invoice price while ignoring the trade terms package—rebates, allowances, payment terms—routinely leaves 300–700 basis points of margin uncaptured; retailers that train buyers on total terms economics consistently outperform on gross margin versus peers who focus solely on unit cost negotiation.

**Core Trade Terms — Definitions, Mechanics, and Settlement**

**Volume Rebate** *(also: Retrospective Rebate, Back-End Rebate)*
- **Definition**: A payment from supplier to retailer calculated as a percentage of total purchases when a pre-agreed annual volume threshold is achieved, typically paid quarterly or annually in arrears.
- **Mechanics**: Rebate tiers are structured as step-ups: the retailer earns 1% on the first $20M of purchases, 2% on purchases between $20M and $40M, and 3% on purchases above $40M. The supplier accrues the liability monthly and either issues a credit note against future invoices or wires payment at the agreed settlement date. If the retailer fails to reach a tier threshold, no rebate is paid for that tier—creating a commercial incentive to concentrate purchases with fewer suppliers to hit higher tiers.
- **Example**: Walmart negotiates tiered volume rebates with major CPG suppliers: 1% at $20M, 2% at $40M, 3% at $80M+ in annual purchases. On a $300M annual supplier relationship, the effective rebate at the top tier represents $9M in margin—an amount larger than most invoice price negotiations.
- **Business Implication**: A buyer who does not model rebate tier thresholds at the start of the fiscal year will miss opportunities to concentrate volume to unlock higher tiers; category managers at Tier 1 retailers typically manage purchase volumes deliberately against rebate step-up points in Q3 and Q4.

**Listing Fee**
- **Definition**: A one-time fee paid by a supplier to a retailer to secure the placement of a new SKU in the retailer's assortment, covering the cost of range review, shelf reset, and system setup.
- **Mechanics**: Listing fees are negotiated at product introduction and are typically non-refundable even if the product is subsequently delisted. The fee is invoiced by the retailer upon range confirmation, before the first purchase order. In practice, listing fees function as a risk-sharing mechanism: the retailer charges the supplier for the shelf space and administrative investment of ranging an unproven product.
- **Example**: Sainsbury's and Tesco charge new branded suppliers listing fees of £2,000–£10,000 per SKU per banner for standard grocery categories. Premium shelf locations (eye-level in high-traffic aisles) command higher listing fees and are negotiated separately from standard ranging.
- **Business Implication**: A brand launching into UK grocery for the first time must budget listing fees as part of launch COGS before projecting any margin; failure to factor listing fees into business case modelling is one of the most common reasons small suppliers underestimate year-one profitability.

**Slotting Fee** *(also: Slotting Allowance, Pay-to-Play Fee)*
- **Definition**: A payment from a supplier to a retailer for dedicated physical shelf space or premium in-store placement, particularly at new product launch, most prevalent in U.S. grocery channels.
- **Mechanics**: Slotting fees are charged per SKU per store (or per chain) and reflect the opportunity cost to the retailer of displacing an existing product and committing to a minimum display period (typically 3–6 months). Fees are typically paid upfront before first ship, though some retailers accept a per-case bill-back structure for new items. Slotting fees are distinct from listing fees in that they compensate for physical shelf commitment, not administrative processing.
- **Example**: A new beverage brand entering Kroger's 2,800 stores may pay $80,000–$150,000 in slotting fees for refrigerated shelf placement. A major CPG company launching a line extension pays lower or zero slotting fees due to existing shelf relationships and promotional investment leverage (FMI, 2023).
- **Business Implication**: A buyer whose category has excess slotting demand from suppliers can use slotting fee revenue to fund category promotions or offset markdown costs; a buyer who waives slotting fees for unproven products without offsetting commercial compensation is foregoing a legitimate margin tool.

**Co-op Advertising Allowance** *(also: Promotional Allowance, Marketing Fund Contribution)*
- **Definition**: A per-unit or percentage-of-sales contribution from the supplier to fund joint advertising, circular features, or digital promotions managed by the retailer.
- **Mechanics**: Co-op is accrued by the retailer as a percentage of purchases (e.g., 2% of invoice cost) and pooled into a promotional fund. The retailer draws against this fund to pay for circular placements, endcap features, digital media, and loyalty program offers. The supplier receives an accounting of how the fund was spent against agreed marketing activities; unspent funds may roll forward or be reclaimed by the retailer at year end depending on contract terms.
- **Example**: Target charges CPG suppliers a 2% "partner marketing fund" contribution which funds Target Circle loyalty offers and digital circular placements. For a supplier with $100M in annual Target sales, this generates $2M in retailer-controlled promotional spend, the allocation of which Target determines based on category priorities.
- **Business Implication**: A supplier that contributes to co-op funds without negotiating specific, measurable activation commitments in return (named weeks, confirmed digital placements, guaranteed circular space) is effectively making an uncapped donation to the retailer's promotional P&L.

**Payment Terms**
- **Definition**: The contractual period within which the retailer pays the supplier's invoice following shipment or delivery confirmation.
- **Mechanics**: Standard payment terms in U.S. retail are Net 30 (full payment 30 days after invoice date). Large retailers extract additional working capital benefit by extending terms to Net 60 or Net 90—effectively using the supplier's balance sheet to finance inventory already sold to consumers. Payment terms are negotiated annually and are a direct function of the retailer's buying power; suppliers with limited alternative distribution channels have less leverage to resist extended terms.
- **Example**: Amazon Vendor Central operates on Net 60 payment terms for most suppliers, effectively financing approximately $2B in supplier payables at any given time. For a small supplier with $5M in annual Amazon sales, Net 60 terms (versus their cost of capital at 8%) represent approximately $66,000 in annual implicit financing cost.
- **Business Implication**: A supplier negotiating with a large retailer should model the working capital cost of extended payment terms against the revenue opportunity; if the retailer demands Net 90, the effective cost of capital should be built into the wholesale price negotiation.

**Early Payment Discount (EPD)** *(also: 2/10 Net 30)*
- **Definition**: A discount offered by the supplier to the retailer for settling an invoice within a shorter window than the agreed standard payment terms.
- **Mechanics**: Structured as "2/10 Net 30"—the retailer earns a 2% discount if it pays within 10 days versus the standard 30-day term. The supplier offers EPD to improve its own cash flow predictability. The retailer must have treasury processes in place to capture EPD systematically; retailers that take EPD inconsistently leave money on the table. At scale, EPD capture is managed by the accounts payable team against a list of supplier-specific discount terms.
- **Example**: P&G offers Walmart a 2% early payment discount on invoices paid within 10 days. On $15B in annual Walmart purchases, systematic EPD capture is worth approximately $300M to Walmart annually—more than many retailers earn from their entire private label margin.
- **Business Implication**: Retailers that implement automated early payment capture processes—where AP systems flag every eligible invoice for accelerated payment—consistently generate 0.5–1.5% incremental gross margin from EPD alone, a return that requires no product change or supplier negotiation.

**Bill-Back Allowance** *(also: Promotional Bill-Back, Scan-Down Allowance)*
- **Definition**: A promotional fund that accrues at a fixed amount per unit sold during a defined promotional period and is subsequently deducted from the supplier's future invoices by the retailer.
- **Mechanics**: At the start of a promotional event, the retailer and supplier agree a per-unit bill-back rate (e.g., $0.50 per case) for all units sold during weeks X through Y. The retailer tracks actual sales during the promotion and generates a deduction credit note at the end of the promotional period. Bill-backs are distinct from invoice deductions taken upfront—they are reconciled against actual sales, meaning the supplier's liability is proportional to the promotion's sell-through.
- **Example**: Costco's promotional allowance structure requires suppliers to fund 100% of any temporary price reduction (TPR) through a bill-back mechanism. For a product temporarily priced at $2.00 below regular shelf price, Costco bills the supplier $2.00 per unit sold during the promotional window, reconciled monthly.
- **Business Implication**: A supplier who does not model promotional bill-back exposure before agreeing to a promotion may discover that high sell-through in a deep-discount event produces a larger-than-anticipated bill-back deduction—effectively making a high-volume promotion P&L-negative; always model bill-back liability at both expected and upside sell-through scenarios.

**Scan-Based Trading (SBT)** *(also: Pay-by-Scan, Consignment)*
- **Definition**: A supply arrangement in which the supplier retains title and ownership of goods placed on the retailer's shelf until the consumer purchase is scanned at point-of-sale, at which point the retailer pays the supplier and takes ownership simultaneously.
- **Mechanics**: Under SBT, the retailer never carries the inventory on its balance sheet. The supplier replenishes based on consumer scans rather than purchase orders, and unsold product remains the supplier's financial liability. SBT is operationally complex—it requires exact scan reconciliation between retailer POS and supplier billing—but eliminates the retailer's inventory investment and markdown risk entirely.
- **Example**: 7-Eleven Japan operates SBT with fresh food suppliers; product remains on the supplier's balance sheet until POS scan. This enables 7-Eleven to carry 500+ fresh SKUs per store with near-zero inventory carrying cost, driving its category-leading fresh food margin.
- **Business Implication**: A category manager evaluating SBT should weigh the balance-sheet benefit (no inventory investment, no markdown risk) against the operational overhead of reconciliation and the supplier relationship implications of transferring all inventory risk to the partner.

**Dead Net Price** *(also: Net Net Price, True Cost)*
- **Definition**: The true per-unit cost to the retailer after all trade term deductions—volume rebates, co-op allowances, early payment discounts, promotional bill-backs—are subtracted from the invoice price.
- **Mechanics**: Dead net is calculated by the retailer's finance or trading team at the close of each fiscal period to reconcile true cost of goods against the gross invoice. It is the only accurate input to margin planning. Without dead net calculation, retailers routinely overstate cost of goods and understate gross margin in category P&L reviews.
- **Example**: Dead net price calculation on a $24.00 invoice case cost:

```
Dead Net Price = Invoice Price − All Trade Term Deductions

Invoice Price:              $24.00
Less Volume Rebate (2%):   − $0.48
Less Co-op (1.5%):         − $0.36
Less EPD (2% if taken):    − $0.48
Less Bill-Back:            − $0.30

Dead Net Price:             $22.38  (6.75% below invoice price)
```

- **Business Implication**: A buyer who reports category margin using invoice price rather than dead net price is overstating cost by 3–8% and making range and promotion decisions based on incorrect economics; dead net reconciliation is mandatory for accurate JBP target-setting and AOP financial planning.

> **Key Takeaway**: The "dead net price" is the only number that matters for true margin calculation. Retail buyers who negotiate only invoice price leave 300–700 basis points of margin on the table that skilled negotiators capture through trade terms.

## 4. Vendor Managed Inventory (VMI)

### 4.1 How VMI Works

**Vendor Managed Inventory (VMI)** *(also: Supplier Managed Inventory, Continuous Replenishment Program — CRP)*
- **Definition**: A supply chain arrangement in which the supplier takes responsibility for monitoring the retailer's inventory levels and initiating replenishment orders within pre-agreed minimum/maximum stocking parameters, using shared point-of-sale and inventory data.
- **Mechanics**: The retailer grants the supplier read access to real-time point-of-sale data and on-hand inventory feeds through a supplier portal or automated data exchange. The supplier's replenishment team uses these demand signals and agreed minimum/maximum parameters to generate replenishment shipments without waiting for a retailer-issued purchase order. The retailer retains the right to accept or reject proposed orders, but in mature VMI relationships this is largely automated. The supplier invoices only upon shipment confirmation.
- **Example**: The Walmart/P&G VMI program, launched in 1987 at a Walmart distribution center in Arkansas, is the canonical industry case. P&G was given access to Walmart's inventory data for Pampers and other categories and took ownership of replenishment decisions. Within 18 months, Walmart's in-stock rate on P&G items improved from 87% to 97%, and inventory turns increased by 40%. The model was subsequently expanded across P&G's entire Walmart business (Harvard Business Review, 1994).
- **Business Implication**: A category manager operating VMI with a trusted Tier 1 supplier can redirect internal replenishment analyst hours toward higher-value category and promotional planning work; the business benefit is both inventory efficiency and internal labor reallocation toward strategic activities.

**VMI Implementation Requirements**:

| Requirement | Retailer Obligation | Supplier Obligation |
|---|---|---|
| Data sharing | Provide daily point-of-sale and on-hand inventory via supplier portal or data feed | Build internal replenishment system to consume and act on data |
| Min/Max agreement | Define acceptable inventory range by SKU and location | Maintain inventory within agreed parameters at all times |
| Lead time agreement | Confirm distribution center receiving capacity | Commit to consistent lead times (typically 2–5 days) |
| Communication infrastructure | Advance shipment notice capability; DC receiving accuracy | Shipment notification process; demand planning system |
| Performance accountability | Monitor compliance to min/max; charge back for out-of-stock events | Accept financial accountability for OOS caused by VMI failures |

**Benefits and Challenges of VMI**:

| Dimension | Benefits | Challenges |
|---|---|---|
| Inventory | 15–35% reduction in retailer safety stock (Gartner, 2023) | Supplier may inflate inventory to protect service level, increasing carrying costs |
| Service level | 2–5% improvement in in-stock rates | Requires accurate, real-time data from retailer — garbage in, garbage out |
| Relationship | Builds data-sharing trust; enables JBP and AOP alignment | Creates dependency on single supplier's judgment and data systems |
| Cost | Reduces retailer replenishment labor; improves DC throughput | Supplier absorbs forecast and replenishment operational cost |
| Risk | Retailer offloads replenishment complexity | If supplier is financially distressed, VMI data integrity and replenishment discipline break down |

> **Key Takeaway**: VMI is not a technology project—it is a trust agreement. Without accurate, timely point-of-sale data from the retailer and genuine accountability from the supplier, VMI defaults to a sophisticated guessing exercise that benefits neither party.

## 5. Dropshipping Models

### 5.1 Retail Dropshipping Mechanics

**Dropshipping** *(also: Direct-to-Consumer Fulfillment, Supplier Fulfillment, DSV — Direct Shipment Vendor)*
- **Definition**: A fulfillment model in which the retailer accepts a consumer order and immediately transmits it to the supplier, who ships the product directly to the consumer under the retailer's brand or packaging, without the product passing through the retailer's distribution network.
- **Mechanics**: The retailer lists the supplier's SKUs on its website (often with an "extended aisle" or "marketplace" designation), captures the order and payment, and transmits the order to the supplier as a consumer-level purchase order. The supplier ships within the retailer's promised delivery window using retailer-branded packaging or a neutral box. The retailer pays the supplier at a pre-agreed wholesale cost (typically invoice price minus a dropship margin of 8–15%), invoicing the consumer at full retail. The retailer never holds the inventory, so there is no inventory investment, no markdown risk, and no distribution center handling cost.
- **Example**: Walmart's "Marketplace" and "Extended Aisle" programs include approximately 100,000+ dropship-eligible SKUs as of 2024. Category expansions in furniture (e.g., Ashley Furniture ships direct from its distribution centers) and large-format outdoor equipment are enabled primarily through dropship because holding these SKUs in Walmart DCs would require excessive cube. Ashley Furniture achieves 3–5 day delivery to Walmart customers with zero Walmart inventory investment (Walmart Seller Center, 2024).
- **Business Implication**: A merchant considering a "long-tail" category extension—adding 500 niche subcategory SKUs—should default to dropship for the first 12 months to test velocity before committing distribution center space and inventory capital to the range; dropship is the correct risk management tool for assortment experimentation.

**When to Use Dropshipping**:

| Scenario | Rationale | Margin Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Long-tail / extended aisle SKUs | Too low velocity to justify DC stocking | Lower margin (supplier retains fulfillment margin): 8–15% lower than DC model |
| Oversized / heavy items | DC handling cost exceeds product margin | Protects category margin by shifting handling cost to supplier |
| New product testing | Avoid inventory risk on unproven SKUs | Margin maintained until velocity justifies DC buy-in |
| Seasonal / limited editions | Avoid end-of-season markdown risk | Supplier absorbs inventory risk entirely |

**Vendor Requirements for Dropship Programs**:
- Pack-and-ship capability from supplier warehouse, not just bulk-pallet shipping
- Retailer-branded packing slip and returns label generation
- Same-day order acknowledgement and within-48-hour shipment confirmation
- Returns management capability (accept and process consumer returns)
- Performance compliance: ≥ 95% ship-on-time rate; ≤ 2% cancellation rate; ≤ 1% defect rate

> **Key Takeaway**: Dropshipping expands assortment depth without inventory capital but requires the supplier to absorb fulfillment capability investment. Retailers must enforce SLA compliance rigorously because consumer experience is entirely in the supplier's hands.

## 6. Joint Business Planning (JBP)

### 6.1 JBP Structure and Process

**Joint Business Planning (JBP)** *(also: Collaborative Business Planning, Strategic Account Planning)*
- **Definition**: A structured annual process in which a retailer and a strategic supplier co-develop a shared business plan with mutually agreed revenue targets, promotional calendars, new product introduction timelines, and investment commitments, reviewed on a quarterly basis against shared KPIs.
- **Mechanics**: JBP is reserved for Tier 1 strategic suppliers (typically the top 20–50 by spend, representing 70–80% of category volume). The process unfolds over 6–8 weeks in Q4 for the following fiscal year: (1) the supplier presents its Category Vision and proposed investment; (2) the retailer's merchant and category manager present their Category Strategy and volume objectives; (3) both parties negotiate a shared P&L that aligns promotional funding, range, and distribution targets; (4) the agreed plan is codified in a JBP document signed by senior leadership from both sides. Quarterly Business Reviews (QBRs) track actuals vs. plan and authorize in-year investment releases.
- **Example**: Unilever's JBP with Tesco UK is conducted at the "Strategic Supplier" level, involving Unilever's Customer Business Development Director and Tesco's Buying Controller. The 2023 JBP included: (a) Tesco-exclusive product variant for Dove Body Wash driving £8M in incremental sales; (b) Unilever's commitment to fund 30% of Tesco's digital media spend for the laundry category; (c) a joint NPD pipeline with 3 new SKU launches aligned to Tesco's "Healthy Living" strategy. Unilever cited Tesco as one of its top 5 global growth customers in 2023 (Unilever Annual Report, 2023).
- **Business Implication**: A retailer that invests in JBP with its top 20 suppliers typically captures 2–4x more discretionary promotional investment than one that negotiates transactionally, because suppliers direct disproportionate investment toward retail partners where they have a co-authored plan with aligned senior management commitment.

**JBP Components**:

| Component | Retailer Input | Supplier Input | Shared Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue Targets | Category growth ambition, space allocation | Volume commitment, distribution targets | Joint revenue goal by category |
| Promotional Calendar | Key promotional windows, price investment periods | Promotional funding commitments, NPD timing | Agreed promotional activity plan |
| Range & Distribution | Ranging decisions, core vs. extended range | Priority SKU list, NPD pipeline | Agreed SKU list and launch dates |
| Investment Plan | In-store activation requests, digital media | Co-op funding, trade investment allocation | Signed investment schedule |
| Shared KPIs | In-stock rate, sales vs. plan, category share | Fill rate, OTIF, NPS on new products | Monthly scorecard against plan |

> **Key Takeaway**: JBP only creates value when both parties bring genuine data, candid category insight, and authority to commit investment. A JBP process where the supplier presents a slide deck and the retailer responds with a wish list is a waste of both organizations' senior management time.

## 7. Annual Operating Plan (AOP)

### 7.1 Retailer-Vendor AOP Process

**Annual Operating Plan (AOP)** *(also: Annual Business Plan, Joint Annual Plan)*
- **Definition**: The financial and operational plan, jointly developed by a retailer and its key vendors, that quantifies the mutual revenue, cost, and investment commitments for the upcoming fiscal year, forming the contractual basis for trade terms and JBP activities.
- **Mechanics**: The AOP process begins 3–4 months before the fiscal year start. The retailer provides category sales targets and promotional investment requirements; the vendor responds with a proposed investment offer (trade terms total value, NPD commitments, supply chain improvement targets). Negotiation occurs over 4–6 weeks to reach a "closed AOP" signed by both parties. The AOP governs: the total trade terms package (value and structure), specific promotional events (weeks, depth, funding source), new product launches (timing, distribution targets, launch support), and supply chain commitments (fill rate targets, lead time improvements).
- **Example**: Procter & Gamble's AOP process with Walmart is conducted annually in Bentonville, Arkansas, at P&G's dedicated Customer Business Development (CBD) office located adjacent to Walmart's headquarters. P&G assigns approximately 300 staff in Bentonville specifically to manage the Walmart relationship—the largest co-location of supplier staff at a retailer in the world. The annual AOP for the P&G/Walmart relationship covers $15B+ in mutual sales and 200+ SKUs across grocery, health, and beauty categories (Forbes, 2023).
- **Business Implication**: A merchant who allows AOP negotiations to conclude in February for a January fiscal year has effectively granted the supplier interest-free deferred trade term contributions for two months—at $50M in annual purchases, a 2% annual rebate deferred by 60 days costs the retailer approximately $166,000 in working capital; AOP closure before fiscal year start is a finance discipline, not just a planning preference.

### 7.2 AOP vs. JBP — The Practitioner Distinction

AOP and JBP are complementary but distinct processes that most Tier 1 retailer-supplier relationships run in parallel on different timelines. Conflating the two leads to incomplete planning and unenforceable financial commitments.

The **AOP** is the financial contract: it locks the specific dollar values of trade terms, the volume thresholds that trigger rebates, the exact weeks and funding levels of promotional events, and the supply chain performance targets with associated penalties. It is signed by finance and commercial leaders and is legally binding.

The **JBP** is the strategic plan: it articulates the shared category vision, the NPD innovation pipeline, the shopper activation strategy, and the distribution ambitions. It is forward-looking and aspirational, providing the commercial rationale that justifies the investment figures embedded in the AOP.

A common failure mode: completing a JBP without a closed AOP leaves the shared plan unfunded. A closed AOP without a JBP produces correct financial commitments with no strategic alignment to make them achievable.

**AOP vs. JBP Comparison**:

| Dimension | AOP | JBP |
|---|---|---|
| Primary purpose | Financial contract — locks investment $ | Strategic plan — aligns category ambition |
| Key outputs | Signed trade terms schedule, volume tiers, promotional funding calendar | Category vision, NPD pipeline, activation calendar |
| Who signs | Finance director (both sides) + commercial lead | Category manager + buyer + supplier KAM |
| When completed | Before fiscal year start (T−1 month) | Q4 of prior year (T−2 to T−3 months) |
| Review cadence | Quarterly financial reconciliation | Quarterly business review (QBR) |
| Enforceability | Legally binding commercial agreement | Commercially binding by relationship convention |
| Failure mode | Late close = deferred investment, disputed accruals | No AOP = unfunded plan; no JBP = funded plan with no strategy |

**AOP Process Timeline**:

| Milestone | Timing (relative to fiscal year start) | Owner | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Retailer issues category volume targets and trade investment requirements | T − 4 months | Merchant / Category Manager | Category brief to supplier |
| Supplier responds with investment proposal and NPD commitments | T − 3 months | Supplier Key Account Manager | Proposed terms package |
| Commercial negotiation and alignment | T − 3 to T − 2 months | Merchant + Supplier CBD team | Agreed terms in principle |
| Legal and finance review | T − 2 months | Finance + Legal (both sides) | Draft AOP agreement |
| AOP closed and signed | T − 1 month | Both parties' commercial directors | Signed AOP document |
| Q1 promotional plan activated | T = Fiscal year start | Operations + Marketing | First promotional execution |
| Mid-year AOP review (in-year adjustment) | T + 6 months | QBR team | Amendment or reconfirmation |

> **Key Takeaway**: The AOP is not a planning document—it is a binding financial commitment. JBP gives it strategic direction; AOP gives it legal enforceability. Retailers that run both processes on disciplined timelines consistently out-invest peers on promotional activation because their investment funds are committed and accessible from day one of the fiscal year.

## 8. Ethical Sourcing

### 8.1 Ethical Sourcing Framework

**Ethical Sourcing** *(also: Responsible Sourcing, Sustainable Procurement)*
- **Definition**: The systematic process by which a retailer ensures its supply chain meets defined standards for labor practices, human rights, environmental impact, and business integrity—verified through supplier audits, certifications, and contractual obligations embedded in Supplier Codes of Conduct.
- **Mechanics**: Ethical sourcing programs operate through four mechanisms: (1) **Supplier Code of Conduct** — a legally binding document outlining minimum standards for labor (no forced labor, no child labor, freely chosen employment, safe working conditions, living wage alignment, freedom of association), environmental (waste management, emissions targets, chemical restrictions), and business ethics (anti-bribery, anti-corruption); (2) **Factory Audits** — scheduled and unannounced audits conducted by the retailer's internal team or third-party auditors (Bureau Veritas, SGS, Intertek) using SMETA, BSCI, or proprietary protocols; (3) **Third-Party Certifications** — SA8000, Fair Trade, Rainforest Alliance, or GOTS that provide independent verification; (4) **Corrective Action Plans (CAPs)** — time-bound remediation commitments for audit findings, categorized as Critical (zero tolerance, immediate suspension), Major (must remediate within 90 days), or Minor (improvement expected within 180 days).
- **Example**: H&M's "Sustainability Commitment" requires all 1,800+ direct suppliers to complete an annual HIGG Facility Environmental Module (FEM) assessment—a standardized environmental self-assessment tool covering energy, water, waste, and chemical management—and achieve a minimum score of 40/100 to remain on the approved vendor list. H&M publishes its full supplier list—1,800 factories in 60+ countries—making it one of the most transparent supply chain disclosure programs in fashion (H&M Sustainability Report, 2023).
- **Business Implication**: A category manager sourcing private label from an unaudited factory carries personal and commercial liability for supply chain violations—the 2013 Rana Plaza collapse established beyond doubt that "I didn't know" is not a defense for retailers or buyers; completing a factory audit before the first purchase order is issued is the minimum professional standard, not an optional compliance step.

**Key Ethical Sourcing Certifications**:

| Certification | Body | Standard Focus | Retailer Application |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SA8000** | Social Accountability International | Labor rights: child labor, forced labor, health & safety, working hours, compensation | Walmart requires SA8000 for all direct-import apparel factories |
| **Fair Trade USA** | Fair Trade USA | Farmer/worker income, community development premiums, environmental standards | Whole Foods requires Fair Trade certification on 30%+ of its coffee assortment |
| **Rainforest Alliance** | Rainforest Alliance | Sustainable agriculture, forest protection, biodiversity | Nestlé (Kit Kat, Nescafé) sources certified cocoa/coffee sold in Tesco, Walmart |
| **GOTS (Global Organic Textile Standard)** | GOTS | Organic fiber processing, social criteria in textile supply chain | IKEA requires GOTS for all organic cotton products in its range |
| **BSCI (Business Social Compliance Initiative)** | amfori | Social performance in global supply chains; audit protocol | Widely used by European retailers including Metro, REWE, and Carrefour |
| **SMETA (Sedex Members Ethical Trade Audit)** | Sedex | 4-pillar audit: labor, health & safety, environment, business ethics | M&S, Tesco, Sainsbury's use SMETA as their primary audit protocol |

**Supplier Audit Process — Step by Step**:

1. **Supplier Self-Assessment**: Supplier completes a questionnaire covering labor practices, safety records, environmental compliance, and management systems.
2. **Document Review**: Auditor reviews payroll records, time sheets, HR policies, environmental permits, and safety inspection reports.
3. **Factory Walk-Through**: Auditor inspects worker dormitories, cafeterias, fire exits, chemical storage, and production floors.
4. **Worker Interviews**: Private interviews with 10–15% of workers (never in management presence) to verify conditions match documentation.
5. **Audit Report & CAP**: Findings classified as Critical/Major/Minor; supplier given timeline for corrective actions.
6. **Re-audit**: Critical findings require re-audit within 30 days; Major findings within 90 days.

> **Key Takeaway**: Ethical sourcing is not a compliance checkbox—it is a supply chain risk management function. A single child labor finding at a Tier 2 supplier (supplier's supplier) can generate front-page reputational damage worth more than the annual trade terms of the entire category.

## 9. Supplier Diversity Programs

### 9.1 Definition, Business Case, and Mechanics

**Supplier Diversity Program** *(also: Diverse Supplier Program, Inclusive Sourcing)*
- **Definition**: A proactive procurement initiative in which a retailer sets quantitative spending targets with businesses that are at least 51% owned and operated by individuals from underrepresented groups, including minority-owned (MBE), women-owned (WBE), veteran-owned (VOSB), LGBTQ+-owned (LGBTBE), and small business enterprises (SBE).
- **Mechanics**: Retailers establish annual supplier diversity spend goals (typically 10–25% of addressable procurement spend) and track them against certifications from recognized bodies—NMSDC (National Minority Supplier Development Council) for MBEs, WBENC (Women's Business Enterprise National Council) for WBEs, and NVBDC for veteran-owned businesses. Category managers are given diversity-spend targets within their category budgets, creating an internal market for qualifying suppliers. Many retailers also operate dedicated "pitch events" or accelerator programs to develop diverse supplier pipelines.
- **Example**: Walmart's "Supplier Inclusion" program set a goal of sourcing $20B from diverse suppliers between 2021 and 2030. By 2023, Walmart had reached $13B toward that goal, with women-owned businesses accounting for $6.2B and minority-owned businesses $4.8B of the total (Walmart ESG Report, 2023). Walmart also operates a "Built for Better" product tag that highlights products from diverse-owned businesses on its digital shelf.
- **Business Implication**: A category manager who receives a diversity spend target but has no pre-qualified pipeline of certified diverse suppliers cannot meet the goal reactively at year-end; maintaining 3–5 approved-but-uncommitted diverse supplier alternatives per category is how diversity targets get met without disrupting supply security.

### 9.2 The Business Case for Supplier Diversity

Supplier diversity is often presented as an ESG compliance commitment, but leading retailers manage it as a commercial growth strategy with four distinct business drivers:

**Innovation pipeline access**: Diverse-owned businesses—particularly regional, ethnic-food, and culturally specific brands—supply products that large CPG manufacturers do not. Target's partnership with BLK & Bold (a Black-owned coffee brand) produced a coffee SKU that outperformed the category velocity index by 2.3x in multicultural markets, an outcome no incumbent CPG supplier was positioned to deliver (Target ESG Report, 2022).

**Market share in high-growth demographics**: Retailers operating in markets with significant minority population concentrations—Hispanic-majority metros, African American urban markets—find that stocking locally owned or culturally relevant diverse brands drives basket conversion among shoppers who self-select stores based on assortment representation. Whole Foods has attributed up to 8% of incremental basket size in targeted stores to culturally relevant diverse-brand introductions (NRF, 2022).

**Concentration risk reduction**: Diverse suppliers, particularly smaller regional manufacturers, often fill supply gaps that national manufacturers cannot service efficiently (small pack sizes, regional flavor preferences, shorter production runs). Kroger's "supplier development" program for diverse vendors reduced single-source concentration in 12 food categories by qualifying diverse-owned alternatives that could step in during supply disruptions (Kroger ESG Report, 2023).

**Regulatory and ESG reporting requirements**: SEC climate disclosure rules (2024) and large-retailer ESG reporting frameworks (GRI, SASB) increasingly require supplier diversity spend disclosure. Retailers that have not built tracking infrastructure face compliance costs at the time of mandatory reporting that far exceed the cost of building the program proactively.

**Supplier Diversity Reporting Metrics**:

| Metric | Formula | Industry Benchmark |
|---|---|---|
| Diversity Spend % | Diverse supplier spend ÷ Total addressable spend | 10–25% goal (Fortune 500 retailers); median ~12% |
| Certified Diverse Supplier Count | Number of NMSDC/WBENC certified vendors on approved list | Target: 8–12% of vendor base |
| New Diverse Supplier Pipeline | New diverse suppliers onboarded ÷ Total new vendors | Target: ≥ 20% of new vendor additions |

> **Key Takeaway**: Supplier diversity programs that lack category manager accountability—tracked spend targets, reviewed quarterly, tied to sourcing performance—remain aspirational; those integrated into sourcing scorecards and category business plans consistently deliver against targets because every buyer has skin in the game.

## 10. Vendor Compliance

### 10.1 Routing Guides, Packaging Standards, and Chargebacks

**Vendor Compliance Program** *(also: Supplier Compliance, Trade Compliance)*
- **Definition**: A formal system of routing instructions, packaging and labeling standards, and financial penalties (chargebacks) designed to ensure suppliers deliver goods in a manner that minimizes the retailer's inbound logistics cost and distribution center processing time.
- **Mechanics**: Retailers publish a **Routing Guide** that specifies: carrier selection (must use retailer-specified carriers or authorized third-party logistics providers), shipment consolidation requirements (full truckload vs. less-than-truckload thresholds), advance shipment notice timing (must transmit before trailer departs supplier dock), pallet configuration (standard vs. floor-loaded), labeling (standardized barcode carton labels meeting retailer specification), and ticketing requirements (pre-ticketed merchandise with retailer UPC and price ticket). Deviations from the routing guide trigger chargebacks—financial deductions against the supplier's invoice—assessed at a fixed dollar amount per violation or a percentage of the shipment value.
- **Example**: Walmart's **OTIF (On Time In Full)** compliance program, implemented in 2017, charges a 3% chargeback on the cost of goods for any purchase order line that falls below the 98.5% OTIF threshold. In fiscal year 2023, Walmart collected an estimated $750M–$900M in OTIF chargebacks from suppliers (Wall Street Journal, 2023). A Procter & Gamble spokesperson confirmed that OTIF penalties represent a "significant cost driver" in its Walmart trade investment (P&G Earnings Call, Q2 2023).
- **Business Implication**: A new supplier that does not invest in routing guide compliance infrastructure—labeling equipment, automated shipment notification, carrier pre-approval—can lose 3–8% of invoice value in chargebacks within the first year, effectively eliminating all trade margin on the account; compliance investment is an ROI-positive spend that pays back within 6–12 months.

**Common Compliance Violations and Chargeback Rates**:

| Violation Type | Description | Typical Chargeback |
|---|---|---|
| Late ASN | Advance shipment notice transmitted after trailer departure | $50–$200 per PO or 1–2% of invoice |
| Missing/incorrect carton label | Standardized barcode label absent, unreadable, or misplaced | $25–$100 per carton |
| Routing violation | Using non-approved carrier or routing without authorization | 2–5% of invoice or flat $500+ per shipment |
| OTIF failure | Below retailer's OTIF threshold (e.g., Walmart < 98.5%) | 3% of cost of goods (Walmart) |
| Ticketing error | Wrong price ticket, missing UPC, or incorrect hang tag | $0.10–$0.50 per unit or $250 per PO |
| Pack non-compliance | Wrong inner pack count or pallet configuration | $100–$500 per pallet or 1% of shipment value |
| Over/under shipment | Quantity variance beyond ±2% of PO | Excess units returned at supplier cost |

> **Key Takeaway**: Chargeback programs are profit centers for large retailers, not just compliance tools. Vendors that do not invest in compliance infrastructure can lose 3–8% of invoice value in chargebacks, effectively eliminating all trade profit from the account.

## 11. Strategic vs. Transactional Suppliers

### 11.1 Supplier Tiering Framework

**Strategic Supplier** *(also: Key Supplier, Preferred Supplier)*
- **Definition**: A vendor relationship managed at the senior executive level, with mutual long-term investment, deep data sharing, and collaborative planning, typically representing the top 5–15% of a retailer's supplier base by spend or strategic criticality.
- **Mechanics**: Strategic suppliers account for 70–80% of category volume and are eligible for JBP, VMI, co-development of private label, and executive-to-executive relationship management. The retailer may co-invest in the supplier's capabilities (e.g., funding dedicated production lines, co-developing exclusive packaging), creating switching barriers on both sides. Category managers are measured on the health of these relationships, not just their cost outcomes.
- **Example**: Costco designates fewer than 500 strategic suppliers globally who collectively represent approximately 85% of its $240B in annual merchandise revenue. Costco's "Kirkland Signature" private label is co-manufactured by strategic suppliers such as Starbucks (Kirkland coffee), Duracell (Kirkland batteries), and Jelly Belly (Kirkland jelly beans)—relationships where Costco's volume effectively subsidizes the supplier's branded manufacturing utilization (Costco Annual Report, 2023).
- **Business Implication**: A merchant who manages a strategic supplier on price alone—re-tendering annually without JBP or co-investment—signals to the supplier that the relationship is expendable, which guarantees the supplier will prioritize their innovation pipeline, exclusive products, and best commercial terms for a competitor retailer instead.

**Transactional Supplier** *(also: Commodity Supplier, Spot Supplier)*
- **Definition**: A vendor relationship managed primarily on price and specification compliance, with minimal strategic collaboration, frequently re-bid against competitors, and substitutable with alternative sources at short notice.
- **Mechanics**: Transactional suppliers are managed via competitive tendering, reverse auctions, and short-term contracts (6–12 months). The buyer's interaction is primarily with a sales coordinator rather than a strategic account team. These suppliers have limited visibility into the retailer's future plans and compete primarily on lowest delivered cost per unit.
- **Example**: Kroger's commodity private-label paper goods program—supplying store-brand napkins, paper plates, and tissue—is managed as purely transactional. Kroger re-tenders the contract annually via reverse auction across 8–12 qualified manufacturers; the incumbent earns no preferential consideration and contracts are awarded on lowest delivered cost per case. No JBP, VMI, or scorecard review is conducted—compliance failures trigger automatic chargebacks with no escalation path. Kroger's explicit expectation in commodity categories is a 5–10% cost reduction per annual bidding cycle (Kroger Procurement Policy, 2022).
- **Business Implication**: A buyer who devotes QBR time, JBP sessions, and senior account management resources to transactional suppliers misallocates capacity from strategic relationships; governance for transactional suppliers should be rules-based—automated chargebacks, competitive re-tendering triggers—not relationship-managed.

**Supplier Tiering Decision Matrix**:

| Dimension | Strategic Tier | Preferred Tier | Approved Tier | Transactional Tier |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| % of category volume | > 30% | 10–30% | 5–10% | < 5% |
| Replaceability | Difficult (6–18 months) | Moderate (3–6 months) | Easy (1–3 months) | Immediate |
| Relationship level | CEO/EVP | VP/Director | Manager | Buyer/Coordinator |
| Planning horizon | 3–5 years | 1–3 years | 1 year | < 1 year |
| Data sharing | Full POS + inventory | POS + purchase history | Purchase history only | Transactional only |
| Investment model | Mutual capital investment | Co-funded promotions | Standard trade terms | Spot pricing |

> **Key Takeaway**: Misclassifying a strategic supplier as transactional—by managing it on price alone—is one of the most common and costly category management errors. It destroys the supplier's willingness to invest in innovation and exclusivity that drives category differentiation.

## 12. Supplier Risk Assessment

### 12.1 Risk Categories and Mitigation Frameworks

**Supplier Risk Assessment** *(also: Supplier Risk Management, Supply Base Risk Profiling)*
- **Definition**: The systematic identification, quantification, and mitigation planning for risks arising from the supply base, including financial instability, geographic concentration, single-source dependency, quality failure, and geopolitical disruption.
- **Mechanics**: Risk assessment is conducted annually for Tier 1 suppliers and upon onboarding for all new vendors. Risks are scored on a probability × impact matrix and categorized as High/Medium/Low. Mitigation strategies include dual sourcing, strategic inventory buffers, financial health monitoring, and contingency contract clauses (force majeure provisions, business continuity plan requirements). The COVID-19 pandemic exposed catastrophic single-source and geographic concentration risks across global retail supply chains, accelerating dual-sourcing and nearshoring programs industry-wide.
- **Example**: Target's "Supplier Risk Monitor" uses a combination of Dun & Bradstreet financial health scores, geographic risk ratings (country risk indices), and internal scorecard data to generate a quarterly risk dashboard for all Tier 1 and Tier 2 suppliers. During the 2021 supply chain crisis, Target identified 142 single-source SKUs representing more than $500M in annual sales and implemented emergency dual sourcing for 89 of them within 6 months, adding $40M in sourcing cost but protecting against an estimated $200M+ in potential stock-out loss (Target Investor Day, 2022).
- **Business Implication**: A supply chain director who has never mapped single-source SKUs by revenue exposure does not know which products are one factory fire away from zero availability; a single risk mapping exercise typically reveals 5–15% of category revenue concentrated in uninsured single-source relationships that can be remediated through a structured dual-sourcing program.

**Supplier Risk Assessment Matrix**:

| Risk Type | Risk Factor | Probability | Impact | Mitigation Strategy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Financial Risk** | Supplier bankruptcy or liquidity crisis | Assessed via D&B financial health score < 60 | High — supply disruption, working capital exposure | Require audited financials; set financial health threshold; dual-source |
| **Geographic Risk** | Single-country sourcing exposure (natural disaster, political instability, trade tariff) | Medium in stable regions; High in conflict zones | High — complete supply disruption | Multi-country sourcing; nearshoring |
| **Single-Source Risk** | Only one qualified supplier for a critical SKU | Low–Medium (depends on SKU) | Critical — zero supply alternative | Qualify second source; build 90-day safety stock |
| **Quality Risk** | Chronic defect rate or regulatory non-compliance | Track via scorecard history | High — recall costs, consumer harm | AQL inspection program; certification requirements |
| **Geopolitical/Tariff Risk** | Trade sanctions, export controls, tariff escalation | Medium (especially US-China) | High — landed cost increase of 15–50%+ | Tariff engineering; country of origin diversification |
| **Concentration Risk** | > 40% of category volume from single supplier | Moderate | High — pricing leverage lost; supply fragility | Category de-concentration target of ≤ 30% single-source |
| **Cybersecurity Risk** | Supplier data breach exposing retailer order and inventory data | Increasing (30% of breaches via third parties) | High — data loss, order disruption | Supplier IT security assessment; SOC 2 requirement |

> **Key Takeaway**: Supplier risk assessment is most valuable when it produces action—a qualified second source, a strategic inventory buffer, or a contract clause with teeth—not when it produces a risk report that sits in a shared drive folder.

## 13. Practical Application — How Leading Retailers Operationalise Supplier Management

### 13.1 Walmart's Supplier Management Architecture

Walmart manages approximately 100,000 active suppliers through a tiered, technology-enabled structure. Its "Luminate" platform (successor to Retail Link, launched 2022) provides suppliers with granular point-of-sale data, on-shelf availability metrics, and replenishment analytics. Walmart's OTIF program—charging 3% of cost of goods for orders falling below 98.5% compliance—generated measurable behavioral change from over 80% of impacted suppliers within 12 months of enforcement (WSJ, 2023). Walmart's "Strategic Supplier Council" convenes its top 50 global suppliers quarterly for shared industry briefings, early NPD access, and policy consultation—a relationship investment that has reduced its top-50 supplier churn rate to near zero over the past decade. For JBP and AOP, Walmart assigns dedicated "customer team" offices in Bentonville that suppliers staff with their own personnel—P&G's 300-person Bentonville team is the largest but Unilever, Nestlé, and Kraft Heinz each maintain permanent Bentonville offices of 50–150 staff.

### 13.2 IKEA's Supplier Development Model (IWAY)

IKEA's approach to supplier management is distinctive in that it is explicitly development-oriented rather than audit-and-penalize-oriented. IKEA's "IWAY" standard (IKEA Way on Purchasing Products, Materials and Services)—first introduced in 2000 and updated to IWAY 6.0 in 2023—is a mandatory code of conduct covering environmental standards (energy efficiency, water use, chemical management, waste reduction), social standards (working hours, wages, worker safety), and forest and cotton sourcing (FSC-certified wood, GOTS-certified organic cotton). Unlike Walmart's compliance-enforcement model, IKEA's supplier management philosophy is co-investment in capability: IKEA assigns supplier development engineers who live with factories for 3–12 months to help meet IWAY requirements, rather than issuing chargebacks for shortfalls.

IKEA operates approximately 1,800 direct suppliers across 50+ countries. As of 2023, 91% of suppliers meet IWAY "Musts" (zero-tolerance requirements), up from 76% in 2018. IKEA's co-investment in supplier solar installations, waste reduction systems, and worker training programs has reduced the average energy cost per supplier by 18% since 2015, improving supplier margin and making IKEA a preferred customer for high-quality factories competing for volume (IKEA Sustainability Report, 2023). The IWAY model reflects a commercial insight: factories that operate efficiently and ethically produce fewer defects, have lower staff turnover, and are more reliable supply chain partners than factories operating at the compliance minimum.

## 14. Key Metrics & Benchmarks

| Metric | Formula | Industry Benchmark | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fill Rate** | Units shipped ÷ Units ordered × 100 | ≥ 98% (top-tier supplier); 94–97% (average) | Measured at line-item or PO level |
| **On-Time Delivery (OTD)** | Shipments on time ÷ Total shipments × 100 | ≥ 97% (best-in-class); 90–95% (average) | On-time = within ±1 day of MABD |

| **Audit CAP Closure Rate** | CAP items closed on time ÷ Total CAP items × 100 | ≥ 90% within agreed timeline | Critical findings: 100% closure required within 30 days |

## 15. Case Studies

### Case Study 1: Walmart and Procter & Gamble — The JBP and VMI Blueprint

**Retailer**: Walmart Inc., United States, mass merchandise / hypermarket
**Challenge**: In the mid-1980s, Walmart was experiencing chronic out-of-stocks on P&G's Pampers diapers—its highest-velocity diaper SKU—due to misaligned forecast cycles, slow purchase-order processing, and adversarial buyer-seller dynamics that prevented data sharing. Neither company had a mechanism to plan jointly, and Pampers' shelf availability was below 87% at Walmart stores.
**Approach**: In 1987, P&G Vice President Lou Pritchett contacted Sam Walton directly to propose a fundamentally different supply model (Pritchett, *Sold!*, 1995). P&G embedded a team in Bentonville and was given direct access to Walmart's point-of-sale and inventory data. P&G took over replenishment decisions for Pampers and other categories using VMI, eliminating the purchase-order-driven replenishment cycle. A Joint Business Planning process was established with shared revenue targets, promotional calendars, and co-investment in new product launches. By 2023, P&G employs approximately 300 staff in Bentonville dedicated exclusively to the Walmart relationship—the largest permanent co-location of supplier staff at any retailer in the world.
**Outcome**: In-stock rates for P&G products at Walmart improved from 87% to 97% within 18 months. Inventory turns for P&G categories at Walmart increased by 40%. The Walmart-P&G relationship generates approximately $15B in annual bilateral sales, representing approximately 17% of Walmart's U.S. consumer packaged goods revenue and 14% of P&G's global net sales (P&G Annual Report, 2023).
**Lesson for the industry**: JBP and VMI generate the most value when initiated at the CEO/EVP level, not the buyer/account manager level. The power of data transparency over transactional secrecy is the foundational insight of modern retailer-supplier collaboration.

### Case Study 2: Unilever and Tesco — Rebuilding JBP After a Public Pricing Dispute

**Retailer**: Tesco PLC, United Kingdom, grocery supermarket
**Challenge**: In October 2016, Unilever attempted to raise wholesale prices by approximately 10% across its Tesco range—including Marmite, Pot Noodle, Dove, and Comfort—citing the post-Brexit sterling devaluation. Tesco refused, and Unilever products were briefly removed from the Tesco website in a public standoff that made international headlines (The Guardian, October 2016). The incident exposed a critical structural gap: despite Unilever being Tesco's largest FMCG supplier at approximately £1.7B in annual UK sales, there was no JBP framework providing a structured mechanism for cost price change negotiations, mutual financial planning, or escalation protocols.
**Approach**: Following resolution of the 2016 dispute, Unilever and Tesco formalized a "Strategic Account JBP" framework in 2017 with four structural elements: (a) an annual JBP document co-signed by Tesco's Category Director and Unilever's Customer Business Development Vice President; (b) a joint category data-sharing agreement giving Unilever access to Tesco's category sales and Clubcard loyalty panel data; (c) a "pricing corridor" mechanism establishing a pre-negotiated process for cost price changes above 3%, requiring 90 days advance notice and joint gross-margin impact review before any change was implemented; (d) a joint NPD pipeline commitment under which Unilever agreed to give Tesco first-mover exclusivity on 2–3 category innovation launches per year across laundry, personal care, and food. By 2021, the JBP had expanded to integrate Tesco Clubcard shopper data for category planning—one of the first structured supplier-retailer data partnerships in European grocery.
**Outcome**: Unilever's UK retail sales at Tesco grew from £1.7B in 2017 to approximately £2.1B by 2022, a 24% increase over the JBP period. The pricing corridor mechanism handled two subsequent cost price negotiations—the 2018 commodity cost spike and the 2021 supply chain inflation cycle—without a single public dispute or product delisting. Tesco's Dove brand category share in personal care grew by 2.3 percentage points between 2018 and 2022 against a flat total category, attributed to joint activation funded through the JBP investment plan. Unilever cited Tesco as one of its top 5 global growth retail customers in its 2022 and 2023 Annual Reports (Unilever Annual Report, 2023).
**Lesson for the industry**: JBP frameworks are most valuable not in smooth trading conditions but as structured conflict-resolution mechanisms during pricing disputes and supply disruptions. Their highest ROI comes from the crises they prevent, not the plans they generate in stable years.

### Case Study 3: Target and Ethical Sourcing — Responding to the Rana Plaza Collapse

**Retailer**: Target Corporation, United States, mass merchandise / discount
**Challenge**: The April 2013 Rana Plaza factory collapse in Bangladesh killed 1,134 garment workers and exposed multiple retailers—including those sourcing from factories in the same industrial complex—to severe reputational and compliance risk. Target had apparel sourced from Bangladesh and faced immediate questions about its supply chain oversight and whether its ethical sourcing program was fit for purpose.
**Approach**: Target signed the "Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh" within weeks of the collapse, committing to independent third-party inspections of all Bangladesh garment factories in its supply chain and mandatory remediation funding. Target expanded its ethical sourcing team from 12 to 40+ staff, instituted unannounced audits (previously only announced audits were conducted), and required all apparel factories globally to complete SMETA audits using Sedex-accredited auditors. Target also introduced a Corrective Action tracking system that required factory-level CAP closure before any new purchase order was issued to a previously audited factory.
**Outcome**: By 2016, Target had audited 100% of its direct-source apparel factories (1,400+ factories in 40+ countries). Factory audit critical findings declined by 62% between 2014 and 2018. Target's annual "Social Compliance Report" (first issued in 2015) became an industry benchmark for supply chain transparency disclosure. Target reduced its Bangladesh factory base from 280 to 180, concentrating volume with higher-compliance factories (Target Corporate Responsibility Report, 2018).
**Lesson for the industry**: Ethical sourcing programs implemented reactively after a crisis cost significantly more—in audit infrastructure, remediation, and factory relationship rebuilding—than proactive programs. Retailers that move first on standards become the preferred customer for the highest-quality factories.

### Case Study 4: Costco and Supplier Diversity — The Kirkland Brand Paradox

**Retailer**: Costco Wholesale Corporation, United States, warehouse club
**Challenge**: Costco's private label strategy requires extreme volume concentration in a small supplier base, creating an inherent tension with supplier diversity goals—diverse-owned businesses with the manufacturing scale to produce Kirkland Signature volumes represent a rare and hard-to-access pipeline.
**Approach**: Costco addressed this by operating supplier diversity across two distinct tracks: (1) an "Innovation Track" for diverse-owned food and consumer goods brands at the $1M–$10M revenue scale, onboarded through Costco's regional "Small Supplier" program in Food & Sundries; and (2) a procurement-level commitment to include diverse-certified brokers, distributors, and service suppliers in competitive tenders for logistics, packaging, and indirect spend categories. For the Innovation Track, diverse-owned specialty food brands such as Partake Foods (founded by Denise Woodard, a Black female entrepreneur) can enter on a regional, limited-time test basis before national rollout, with Costco buyer support through the scaling process.
**Outcome**: Partake Foods grew from a $2M regional specialty brand to a $20M+ nationally distributed brand through Costco's program within 3 years of onboarding, achieving distribution in 200+ Costco warehouses. Costco reported $3.2B in total diverse supplier spend in fiscal 2023, representing approximately 1.4% of total merchandise spend—lower than mass market peers but on an accelerating trajectory (Costco ESG Report, 2023).
**Lesson for the industry**: Warehouse club formats require a separate supplier diversity playbook because volume thresholds that enable national brands are inaccessible to most diverse-owned businesses. Tiered programs that create structured entry points at different revenue scales are more effective than a single spend target applied uniformly across all procurement categories.

## 16. Glossary

*All key terms from this document, alphabetically sorted. Each definition is standalone — a reader should not need to read the full document to understand a glossary entry.*

| Term | Definition | Also Known As |
|---|---|---|
| **Annual Operating Plan (AOP)** | The jointly developed financial and operational plan between a retailer and key vendor quantifying mutual revenue, investment, and supply chain commitments for the upcoming fiscal year; forms the contractual basis for trade terms and promotional funding. | Annual Business Plan, Joint Annual Plan |
| **Approved Vendor List (AVL)** | The official registry of suppliers authorized to ship product to a retailer, maintained by the merchandising or procurement function as the gating document for all purchase order issuance. | Preferred Supplier List, Approved Supplier List |
| **Advance Shipment Notice (ASN)** | A standardized notification transmitted by the supplier before a shipment departs their facility, detailing contents by carton and pallet to enable distribution center receiving automation and inventory accuracy. | Pre-shipment Notification |
| **AQL (Acceptable Quality Level)** | The maximum defect rate considered acceptable during a quality inspection sample of an inbound shipment; expressed as a percentage of units inspected (e.g., AQL 1.0 means no more than 1% defects in a standard sample). | Acceptance Quality Limit |
| **Bill-Back Allowance** | A promotional fund that accrues at a fixed per-unit rate during a defined promotional period and is subsequently deducted from the supplier's future invoices by the retailer; settles against actual units sold rather than units ordered. | Promotional Bill-Back, Scan-Down Allowance |
| **Business Continuity Plan (BCP)** | A documented plan required of strategic suppliers specifying how they will maintain product supply to the retailer in the event of a facility disruption, natural disaster, or operational failure; a standard contract clause in strategic supplier agreements. | Contingency Plan, Disaster Recovery Plan |
| **Chargeback** | A financial deduction applied by a retailer against a supplier's invoice for non-compliance with routing guides, labeling standards, OTIF thresholds, or quality requirements; enforced without requiring the supplier's approval. | Deduction, Vendor Penalty |
| **Co-op Advertising Allowance** | A per-unit or percentage-of-sales contribution from a supplier to fund joint marketing activities managed by the retailer, including digital media, circular features, loyalty program offers, and in-store activation. | Promotional Allowance, Marketing Fund Contribution |
| **Corrective Action Plan (CAP)** | A time-bound, supplier-committed remediation plan addressing findings from a compliance or ethical sourcing audit, with specific actions, accountable owners, and completion deadlines; classified by finding severity (Critical, Major, Minor). | CAPA (Corrective Action and Preventive Action) |
| **Dead Net Price** | The true per-unit cost to the retailer after all trade term deductions — volume rebates, co-op allowances, early payment discounts, and promotional bill-backs — are subtracted from the supplier's invoice price. | Net Net Price, True Cost |
| **Defect Rate** | The percentage of inbound units that fail to meet the retailer's quality acceptance standard upon inspection at the distribution center, measured as units rejected divided by total units received. | Quality Failure Rate |
| **Dropshipping** | A fulfillment model in which the supplier ships products directly to the consumer on behalf of the retailer, with the retailer never taking physical possession of the inventory; used for extended-aisle, oversized, or low-velocity SKUs. | Supplier Fulfillment, Direct Fulfillment, DSV (Direct Shipment Vendor) |
| **Dual Sourcing** | A supply base risk mitigation strategy in which a retailer qualifies and actively uses two independent suppliers for the same SKU or category, ensuring continuity of supply if one source fails; applied to high-revenue or single-source-risk SKUs. | Second Source, Multi-sourcing |
| **Early Payment Discount (EPD)** | A discount offered by the supplier to the retailer for settling an invoice within a shorter period than the standard payment terms, typically structured as "2% discount if paid within 10 days versus standard 30-day terms." | 2/10 Net 30, Settlement Discount |
| **EDI (Electronic Data Interchange)** | The standardized electronic exchange of business documents — purchase orders, invoices, shipment notices, inventory feeds — between retailer and supplier using agreed data formats; enables automation of order and payment processing. | Electronic Trading, B2B Data Exchange |
| **Ethical Sourcing** | The systematic process ensuring a retailer's supply chain meets defined standards for labor rights, environmental impact, and business ethics, verified through supplier audits, third-party certifications, and legally binding Supplier Codes of Conduct. | Responsible Sourcing, Sustainable Procurement |
| **Fill Rate** | The percentage of ordered units that the supplier actually ships on a given purchase order, measured at line-item, order, or case level; a core vendor scorecard KPI with industry best-practice threshold of ≥ 98%. | Order Fill Rate, Case Fill Rate |
| **GFSI (Global Food Safety Initiative)** | A benchmarking organization that recognizes food safety management certification schemes (SQF, BRC, FSSC 22000, IFS) meeting a defined standard; retailers requiring "GFSI-recognized certification" from food suppliers accept any of these equivalent standards. | Global Food Safety Initiative |
| **HIGG FEM (Facility Environmental Module)** | A standardized self-assessment tool developed by the Sustainable Apparel Coalition measuring a manufacturing facility's environmental performance across energy, water, waste, and chemical management; used by H&M, Gap, and others as an annual supplier environmental benchmark. | Higg FEM |
| **Joint Business Planning (JBP)** | A structured annual process in which a retailer and strategic supplier co-develop a shared business plan with mutual revenue targets, promotional calendars, NPD timelines, and investment commitments, reviewed quarterly against shared KPIs. | Collaborative Business Planning, Strategic Account Planning |
| **Listing Fee** | A one-time fee paid by a supplier to secure placement of a new SKU in a retailer's assortment, covering the cost of range review, shelf reset, and system setup; most prevalent in European grocery channels. | New Product Fee, Range Entry Fee |
| **MABD (Must Arrive By Date)** | The latest acceptable date on which a supplier's shipment must be received at the retailer's distribution center for a given purchase order, used as the reference point for on-time delivery measurement. | Must-Arrive Date, Delivery Due Date |
| **Nearshoring** | The supply chain risk mitigation strategy of relocating production or sourcing from distant overseas markets (e.g., China) to geographically closer countries (e.g., Mexico for U.S. retailers, Eastern Europe for EU retailers) to reduce lead times and geopolitical exposure. | Reshoring (when moved back to domestic), Friend-shoring |
| **On-Time Delivery (OTD)** | The percentage of inbound supplier shipments that arrive at the retailer's distribution center within the agreed delivery window — typically ±1 day of the Must Arrive By Date (MABD); a core vendor scorecard KPI with best-practice threshold of ≥ 97%. | On-Time Shipping (OTS) when measured at origin |
| **Open Book Costing** | A commercial negotiation approach in which the supplier shares its full cost structure — raw materials, labor, overhead, and margin — with the retailer, enabling the retailer to negotiate on the basis of actual cost build-up rather than market price alone; standard practice at Costco for Kirkland Signature sourcing. | Cost Transparency, Open Book Negotiation |
| **OTIF (On Time In Full)** | A composite supply chain KPI measuring the percentage of purchase order lines delivered both on time (within ±1 day of MABD) and in the correct quantity; Walmart's published threshold is 98.5%, with a 3% cost-of-goods chargeback applied below that threshold. | On Time In Full, Perfect Order Rate |
| **Payment Terms** | The contractual period within which the retailer pays the supplier's invoice following shipment confirmation or delivery; standard terms are Net 30 (30 days from invoice), with large retailers commonly negotiating Net 60–90 to maximize working capital. | Net Terms, Invoice Payment Terms |
| **Performance Improvement Plan (PIP)** | A formal, time-bound program issued by a retailer to a supplier that has fallen below scorecard thresholds, specifying required performance improvements, measurement criteria, and the consequence of failure to remediate within 60–90 days. | Supplier Recovery Plan, Vendor PIP |
| **Quarterly Business Review (QBR)** | A scheduled structured meeting between retailer merchant/supply chain leadership and supplier commercial/operations teams to review scorecard performance, discuss category trends, track JBP and AOP progress, and authorize in-year investment releases. | Business Review, Supplier Review |
| **RFP / RFQ (Request for Proposal / Request for Quotation)** | Formal documents issued by a retailer's procurement or buying team to prospective suppliers, soliciting detailed proposals (RFP) or specific price quotes (RFQ) as the first step in a competitive vendor selection process; RFPs are used for complex or strategic sourcing; RFQs for commodity and transactional sourcing. | Tender Document, Sourcing Brief |
| **Routing Guide** | The retailer's published operational document specifying carrier requirements, shipment configuration, advance shipment notice timing, labeling standards, pallet configuration, and ticketing requirements for all inbound freight; the reference document against which all compliance chargebacks are assessed. | Supplier Routing Guide, Compliance Manual |
| **SA8000** | An internationally recognized social accountability certification standard (Social Accountability International) covering nine social performance criteria: child labor, forced labor, health and safety, freedom of association, discrimination, disciplinary practices, working hours, compensation, and management systems. | Social Accountability 8000 |
| **Scan-Based Trading (SBT)** | A supply arrangement in which the supplier retains ownership of goods placed on the retailer's shelf until the consumer purchase is scanned at point-of-sale, at which point title transfers and the retailer's payment obligation is triggered. | Pay-by-Scan, Consignment |
| **Slotting Fee** | A payment from a supplier to a retailer for dedicated physical shelf space or premium product placement at new product launch; most prevalent in U.S. grocery channels, ranging from $2,000 to $150,000+ per SKU per chain depending on category and shelf position. | Slotting Allowance, Pay-to-Play Fee |
| **SMETA (Sedex Members Ethical Trade Audit)** | A widely adopted four-pillar ethical trade audit protocol managed by Sedex, covering labor practices, health and safety, environment, and business ethics; used by M&S, Tesco, Sainsbury's, and hundreds of global retailers as their primary factory audit standard. | Sedex Audit, SMETA 4-Pillar |
| **Supplier Code of Conduct** | A legally binding document embedded in supplier contracts establishing the minimum standards for labor practices, environmental compliance, and business ethics that all suppliers must meet and maintain to retain approved status. | Supplier Standards, Vendor Code of Conduct |
| **Supplier Diversity Program** | A procurement initiative setting quantitative spend targets with businesses at least 51% owned by individuals from underrepresented groups — including minority-owned (MBE), women-owned (WBE), veteran-owned (VOSB), and LGBTQ+-owned (LGBTBE) businesses — tracked against recognized certifications from NMSDC, WBENC, and NVBDC. | Diverse Supplier Program, Inclusive Sourcing |
| **Supplier Risk Assessment** | The systematic identification and mitigation planning for risks arising from the supply base, including financial instability, geographic concentration, single-source dependency, quality failure, and geopolitical disruption; conducted annually for Tier 1 suppliers and at onboarding for all new vendors. | Supply Base Risk Profiling, Vendor Risk Management |
| **Tiered Supplier Model** | A classification system that divides suppliers into relationship tiers (Strategic, Preferred, Approved, Transactional) based on volume concentration, strategic criticality, and replaceability, governing the investment of relationship management resources and data-sharing access. | Supplier Segmentation, Supplier Stratification |
| **Trade Terms** | The contractual financial arrangements between a retailer and supplier governing the total commercial relationship, including invoice price, volume rebates, promotional allowances, payment timing, and other contributions; negotiated annually as part of the AOP process. | Commercial Terms, Supplier Terms |
| **Vendor Managed Inventory (VMI)** | A supply chain arrangement in which the supplier takes responsibility for monitoring the retailer's inventory levels and initiating replenishment within pre-agreed minimum/maximum stocking parameters, using shared point-of-sale and inventory data provided by the retailer. | Supplier Managed Inventory, Continuous Replenishment Program (CRP) |
| **Vendor Scorecard** | A structured measurement tool quantifying supplier operational performance across KPIs (fill rate, on-time delivery, quality, compliance) on a monthly or quarterly cadence, producing a composite score that drives relationship tier classification and commercial consequences. | Supplier Report Card, Supplier Performance Scorecard |
| **Volume Rebate** | A retrospective payment from supplier to retailer, calculated as a percentage of total annual purchases when a pre-agreed volume threshold is achieved; typically structured in ascending tiers to incentivize purchase concentration with the supplier. | Retrospective Rebate, Back-End Rebate |
