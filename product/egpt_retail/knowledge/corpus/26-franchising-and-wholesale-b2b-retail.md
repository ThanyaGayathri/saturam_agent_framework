## Executive Summary

Franchising and wholesale/B2B retail represent two structurally distinct but commercially significant models that together account for a substantial share of global retail activity. Franchising — in which a brand licences its systems, standards, and intellectual property to independent operators in exchange for fees and royalties — enables rapid network expansion with minimal franchisor capital, but requires sophisticated compliance infrastructure to maintain brand consistency at scale. The IFA estimates that franchise businesses generate $860B+ in annual US economic output across 800,000+ franchise establishments (IFA, 2024), with QSR, convenience, and personal services as the dominant sectors.

Wholesale and B2B retail operate on fundamentally different economics than consumer retail: lower gross margins (10–25% vs. 30–60% in consumer retail), higher average order values ($500–$50,000+ per transaction), longer sales cycles, and relationship-driven purchasing decisions that make account management the primary commercial lever. The rise of B2B e-commerce — projected to reach $3.0 trillion in US B2B e-commerce sales by 2027 (Forrester, 2024) — is transforming wholesale distribution, compressing intermediary margins, and forcing traditional distributors to compete on value-added services rather than product access alone.

**Why this matters**: A retail professional who understands franchise economics can evaluate whether to grow a brand through company-owned stores (capital-intensive, full margin capture) or franchising (capital-light, royalty income). A merchant who understands B2B pricing dynamics can negotiate better terms with wholesale suppliers, structure trade customer programmes that drive volume without destroying margin, and identify which distribution model — direct-to-retailer vs. through distribution — maximises category profitability. These are not niche topics; franchising and B2B commerce are the structural backbone of how most retail brands reach scale.

## 1. Franchising Fundamentals

### 1.1 What Is Franchising

**Franchising** *(also: Franchise System, Franchise Model)*
- **Definition**: A contractual business arrangement in which a **franchisor** (the brand owner) grants a **franchisee** (an independent operator) the right to operate a business under the franchisor's brand, systems, and standards in exchange for an initial franchise fee and ongoing royalties, with the franchisee bearing the capital investment and operating risk.
- **Mechanics**: Franchising creates a principal-agent relationship governed by the **Franchise Agreement** — a legally binding contract specifying territory rights, brand standards, fee obligations, training requirements, renewal terms, and termination conditions. The franchisor provides the brand, operating system, training, and ongoing support; the franchisee provides the capital, labour, and local market execution. The franchisor's revenue model is royalty-based (typically 4–8% of gross sales) rather than profit-based, making the franchisor's income relatively insulated from franchisee operating cost fluctuations. The FTC's Franchise Rule (16 CFR Part 436) requires franchisors to provide a **Franchise Disclosure Document (FDD)** to prospective franchisees at least 14 calendar days before any agreement is signed or money changes hands.
- **Example**: McDonald's Corporation operates 40,275 restaurants globally (FY2023), of which 95% are franchised. McDonald's franchisees invest $1.0–$2.3M per location (equipment, leasehold improvements, initial inventory, franchise fee) and pay McDonald's a 4% royalty on gross sales plus rent of approximately 10–12% of sales for McDonald's-owned real estate. McDonald's franchisor revenue of approximately $15B annually (royalties + rent + fees) is generated with minimal capital investment in restaurant operations — the franchisee bears the $1–2.3M per-unit capital cost (McDonald's Annual Report, 2023).
- **Business Implication**: A brand evaluating franchising vs. company-owned expansion must model the trade-off: company-owned stores capture 100% of unit-level operating profit (typically 15–25% of sales in QSR) but require $1–3M per unit in capital; franchised stores generate only 4–8% royalty income but require zero capital per unit. At scale, franchising generates superior return on invested capital (ROIC) for the franchisor — McDonald's ROIC of approximately 30–35% reflects the capital-light franchise model.

**Franchisor vs. Franchisee**
- **Definition**: The **franchisor** is the brand owner who develops and licences the franchise system; the **franchisee** is the independent operator who invests capital to operate a franchise unit under the franchisor's brand and systems.
- **Mechanics**: The relationship is asymmetric: the franchisor sets standards, controls the brand, and earns royalties regardless of franchisee profitability; the franchisee bears operating risk and earns the residual profit after royalties, rent, food/product cost, labour, and other operating expenses. Franchisee operating margins in QSR average 10–20% of gross sales after all fees — meaning a franchisee operating a $1.5M annual revenue McDonald's location earns approximately $150,000–$300,000 in pre-tax operating income. The franchisor-franchisee relationship is governed by the Franchise Agreement and the **Operations Manual** (the proprietary playbook for running the business), which the franchisee must follow but does not own.
- **Example**: Subway's franchise model — 37,000+ locations globally, 100% franchised — illustrates the tension in the franchisor-franchisee relationship. Subway's franchisees pay an 8% royalty + 4.5% advertising fund contribution = 12.5% of gross sales to Subway, among the highest total fee burden in QSR. Subway's average unit volume (AUV) of approximately $450,000 (2023) — significantly below McDonald's $3.6M AUV — means franchisees pay approximately $56,000 annually in fees on a $450,000 revenue base, leaving limited margin for operating profit. Subway's franchisee satisfaction scores rank among the lowest in the IFA's annual survey, reflecting the structural tension between Subway's fee model and franchisee economics (Franchise Business Review, 2024; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A prospective franchisee must model unit economics before signing: total fee burden (royalty + advertising fund + technology fee) of 10–15% of gross sales, combined with food/product cost (25–35%), labour (25–35%), and occupancy (8–12%), leaves 10–20% for operating profit. Franchisees who enter systems with high fee burdens and low AUVs are structurally unprofitable regardless of operational excellence.

**History and Evolution of Franchising**
- **Definition**: Franchising as a modern business model originated in the 1850s with Singer Sewing Machine Company's dealer network, evolved through the 1950s–1960s QSR expansion (McDonald's, KFC, Subway), and has since expanded to encompass 300+ industry sectors including healthcare, fitness, education, and professional services.
- **Mechanics**: Three eras define modern franchising: (1) **Product/trade name franchising** (1850s–1950s) — manufacturers (Singer, Ford, Coca-Cola bottlers) licensed dealers to distribute their products; (2) **Business format franchising** (1950s–present) — franchisors license a complete business system including brand, operations, training, and marketing (McDonald's, Subway, 7-Eleven); (3) **Service franchising** (1980s–present) — expansion into non-food sectors including fitness (Anytime Fitness, 5,000+ locations), healthcare (Massage Envy, 1,100+ locations), and education (Kumon, 26,000+ locations globally). The IFA reports that franchise businesses employ approximately 8.7 million workers in the US and contribute $860B+ to US GDP (IFA, 2024).
- **Example**: 7-Eleven's evolution from a company-owned convenience store chain to a franchise-dominant model illustrates the strategic shift toward franchising. 7-Eleven converted approximately 6,000 company-owned US stores to franchise operations between 2000 and 2020, reducing its capital intensity while maintaining brand control. 7-Eleven's current US network of 13,000+ stores is approximately 95% franchised, generating royalty income of approximately $1.5–2.0B annually with minimal company capital at risk (7-Eleven FDD, 2023; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A brand that has proven its unit economics in company-owned stores should evaluate franchising as its primary growth vehicle once AUV exceeds $500,000 and the operating system is sufficiently documented to be replicated by independent operators. The decision to franchise is effectively irreversible in practice — once franchisees have invested capital, the franchisor cannot easily reclaim operational control without buying back franchise agreements at significant cost.

> **Key Takeaway**: Franchising is fundamentally a capital allocation decision. Franchisors who franchise aggressively achieve network scale 5–10x faster than company-owned expansion at a fraction of the capital cost, but sacrifice unit-level profit margin for royalty income. The optimal franchise/company-owned mix depends on the brand's capital position, operational complexity, and the strategic value of direct market control.

## 2. Franchise Types

### 2.1 Franchise Structure Models

**Unit Franchise** *(also: Single-Unit Franchise)*
- **Definition**: A franchise agreement granting a franchisee the right to operate a single franchise location within a defined territory, the most common franchise structure representing approximately 60% of all franchise agreements.
- **Mechanics**: Unit franchises are the entry point for most franchisees — lower capital requirement ($50,000–$500,000 initial investment depending on the brand), limited operational complexity (one location), and a defined territory that protects the franchisee from direct competition from the same brand. The franchisor retains the right to open additional units in adjacent territories or to grant those rights to other franchisees. Unit franchise agreements typically run 10–20 years with renewal options, subject to the franchisee meeting performance standards.
- **Example**: A Chick-fil-A unit franchise requires an initial fee of only $10,000 — the lowest in QSR — but Chick-fil-A retains ownership of the restaurant building and equipment (valued at $500,000–$1.5M), leasing them to the franchisee. Chick-fil-A's selection process accepts fewer than 1% of applicants (approximately 80 new operators selected from 60,000+ annual applicants), creating an operator quality that drives Chick-fil-A's AUV of $8.7M — the highest in QSR. Chick-fil-A's royalty of 15% of gross sales + 50% of net profits is the highest fee structure in QSR, but franchisees earn approximately $200,000–$300,000 annually on a $8.7M AUV — a strong return on the $10,000 initial investment (Chick-fil-A FDD, 2023; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A first-time franchisee evaluating unit franchise opportunities should prioritise systems with AUVs above $1M and total fee burdens below 10% of gross sales — the combination that generates the most reliable path to a 5–7 year payback on initial investment. Unit franchise agreements with no territory protection are high-risk: a franchisor who opens a second unit 0.5 miles away can halve the first franchisee's sales with no legal recourse.

**Multi-Unit Franchise** *(also: Multi-Unit Operator, MUO)*
- **Definition**: A franchise arrangement in which a single franchisee operates two or more franchise locations, typically under a **Multi-Unit Development Agreement** that commits the franchisee to open a specified number of units within a defined timeframe in exchange for territorial exclusivity.
- **Mechanics**: Multi-unit franchising has become the dominant growth model for major franchise systems: approximately 54% of all US franchise units are operated by multi-unit franchisees (IFA, 2024). Multi-unit operators achieve economies of scale in management (one regional manager overseeing 5–10 locations), purchasing (volume discounts on supplies), and marketing (shared local advertising costs). The franchisor benefits from working with fewer, more sophisticated operators who have proven operational capability. Multi-unit development agreements typically require the franchisee to open 3–10 units over 3–5 years, with territorial exclusivity contingent on meeting the development schedule.
- **Example**: Flynn Restaurant Group — the largest franchise operator in the US — operates 2,355 franchise locations across Applebee's, Arby's, Panera Bread, Pizza Hut, Taco Bell, and Wendy's, generating approximately $4.5B in annual system revenue. Flynn's scale enables it to negotiate preferred vendor pricing (estimated 3–5% below single-unit operator costs), centralise management functions (HR, accounting, marketing), and invest in technology (labour scheduling, inventory management) that individual unit operators cannot afford. Flynn's operating margin of approximately 8–10% — above the industry average of 6–8% for franchise operators — reflects the scale advantages of multi-unit operation (Flynn Restaurant Group investor presentations; approximate benchmark (2024); see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A franchisee who operates 5+ units in a single brand achieves a structural cost advantage over single-unit operators — shared management overhead, volume purchasing, and centralised back-office functions reduce operating costs by 2–4 percentage points. Franchisors should actively recruit multi-unit operators for new market development because they bring capital, management depth, and operational discipline that single-unit operators cannot match.

**Area Development Franchise** *(also: Area Development Agreement, ADA)*
- **Definition**: A franchise agreement granting a franchisee the exclusive right to develop a specified number of franchise units within a defined geographic territory over a set timeframe, combining territorial exclusivity with a development commitment.
- **Mechanics**: Area development agreements differ from multi-unit agreements in their exclusivity structure: the area developer has the exclusive right to develop all franchise units within the territory — no other franchisee can open units in that area. In exchange, the area developer pays a **development fee** (typically $5,000–$25,000 per unit committed) upfront and commits to a development schedule. Failure to meet the development schedule typically results in loss of exclusivity for undeveloped portions of the territory. Area development is the preferred model for brands entering new geographic markets — the area developer provides local market knowledge and capital while the franchisor maintains brand control.
- **Example**: Anytime Fitness uses area development agreements to expand into new markets. An area developer for a mid-sized US metro market (e.g., Raleigh-Durham, NC) might commit to opening 15 Anytime Fitness locations over 5 years, paying a development fee of $150,000 upfront ($10,000 × 15 units) plus the standard franchise fee ($42,500) and royalty (7% of gross sales) for each location. The area developer's exclusivity protects their investment — no competing Anytime Fitness franchisee can open in their territory. Anytime Fitness's 5,000+ global locations are predominantly developed through area development agreements, enabling rapid international expansion (Anytime Fitness FDD, 2023; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A franchisor entering a new geographic market should use area development agreements rather than selling individual unit franchises — the development fee provides upfront capital, the development schedule ensures market coverage, and the area developer's local knowledge reduces the franchisor's market entry risk. The trade-off is reduced franchisor control: if the area developer underperforms, the franchisor cannot easily replace them without buying back the development rights.

**Master Franchise** *(also: Master Franchisee, Sub-Franchisor)*
- **Definition**: A franchise arrangement in which a **master franchisee** acquires the rights to an entire country or large region, with the authority to sub-franchise to individual unit operators within that territory, effectively acting as the franchisor within their territory.
- **Mechanics**: Master franchising is the primary model for international expansion. The master franchisee pays a **master franchise fee** (typically $100,000–$1M+ depending on territory size) and commits to developing a minimum number of units. The master franchisee then recruits and supports sub-franchisees, collecting sub-franchise fees and a portion of sub-franchisee royalties (typically 50% of the royalty, with the other 50% remitted to the original franchisor). The master franchisee bears the cost of adapting the brand to local market conditions (menu localisation, regulatory compliance, marketing translation) and building the local support infrastructure.
- **Example**: Subway's international expansion is built on master franchise agreements. Subway's master franchisee for India — Doctor's Associates Inc. (Subway's parent) granted the rights to Everstone Group — is responsible for developing Subway's 700+ India locations, recruiting and supporting sub-franchisees, and adapting the menu to Indian preferences (no beef, vegetarian options, local flavour profiles). The master franchisee retains approximately 50% of the 8% royalty (4% of gross sales) from Indian sub-franchisees, with the remaining 4% remitted to Subway's global headquarters. Master franchise agreements enable Subway to expand internationally without building local support infrastructure — the master franchisee bears that cost (Subway FDD, 2023; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A franchisor granting master franchise rights to an entire country is effectively delegating brand stewardship to a third party — if the master franchisee fails to maintain brand standards or goes bankrupt, the franchisor's brand in that market is damaged and recovery requires expensive litigation or buyback. Franchisors should require master franchisees to demonstrate $5M+ in liquid capital, prior multi-unit franchise operating experience, and local market infrastructure before granting country-level rights.

> **Key Takeaway**: The franchise structure chosen determines the balance of capital risk, territorial control, and operational complexity for both franchisor and franchisee. Unit franchising minimises franchisee capital risk but limits scale; master franchising enables rapid international expansion but transfers significant brand control to the master franchisee. Franchisors that grow too quickly through master franchise agreements risk brand inconsistency in markets where they have limited oversight.

## 3. Franchise Disclosure Document (FDD)

### 3.1 FDD Structure and Legal Requirements

**Franchise Disclosure Document (FDD)**
- **Definition**: A legally mandated disclosure document that franchisors must provide to prospective franchisees at least 14 calendar days before any franchise agreement is signed or any money is paid, containing 23 standardised items of information about the franchisor, the franchise system, and the financial obligations of the franchisee, as required by the FTC Franchise Rule (16 CFR Part 436).
- **Mechanics**: The FDD is not a contract — it is a disclosure document. The actual binding agreement is the Franchise Agreement, which is attached as an exhibit to the FDD. The FDD must be updated annually within 120 days of the franchisor's fiscal year end and whenever a material change occurs. Franchisors who fail to provide the FDD within the required timeframe, or who provide materially false or misleading information, are subject to FTC enforcement actions and private lawsuits. Many states (California, Illinois, Maryland, Minnesota, New York, and 14 others) have additional franchise registration requirements — franchisors must register their FDD with the state before offering franchises.
- **Example**: McDonald's FDD (2023 edition) is approximately 400 pages and includes audited financial statements for McDonald's Corporation, a list of all current and former franchisees (with contact information), Item 19 financial performance representations showing AUV data for company-owned and franchised restaurants, and the complete Franchise Agreement. Prospective McDonald's franchisees are required to review the FDD with an attorney and accountant before signing — McDonald's recommends a minimum 30-day review period despite the 14-day legal minimum (McDonald's FDD, 2023).
- **Business Implication**: A prospective franchisee who does not engage a franchise attorney to review the FDD is making a $500,000–$2.5M investment without professional due diligence. The FDD contains critical information — including the litigation history of the franchisor, the financial performance of existing franchisees, and the specific obligations that can trigger franchise termination — that is not available from any other source.

### 3.2 Key FDD Items for Prospective Franchisees

The 23 FDD items vary in importance. The following are the items that experienced franchise attorneys and the IFA identify as most critical for prospective franchisee evaluation:

**Item 1 — The Franchisor and Any Parents, Predecessors, and Affiliates**
- **What it contains**: The franchisor's business history, corporate structure, and the business experience of its principals. Reveals whether the franchisor has changed ownership, gone through bankruptcy, or has affiliated entities that supply products to franchisees (a potential conflict of interest).
- **What to look for**: Franchisors with less than 5 years of operating history have limited track records; franchisors with recent ownership changes may have shifted strategic priorities; affiliated supplier relationships may indicate that franchisees are required to purchase from franchisor-controlled entities at above-market prices.
- **Business Implication**: A franchisor acquired by private equity within the past 3 years is a higher-risk investment — PE ownership typically accelerates unit growth targets and fee extraction to generate returns, which can conflict with franchisee profitability. Franchisees should research the PE firm's track record with other franchise investments before signing.

**Item 3 — Litigation**
- **What it contains**: All pending and prior litigation involving the franchisor, its officers, and its affiliates, including franchisee lawsuits, regulatory actions, and criminal convictions.
- **What to look for**: A pattern of franchisee lawsuits (particularly for fraud, misrepresentation, or breach of contract) is a significant red flag. Subway's FDD historically disclosed hundreds of franchisee lawsuits — a volume that reflects systemic franchisee dissatisfaction rather than isolated disputes.
- **Business Implication**: A franchisor with 10+ active franchisee lawsuits is signalling systemic relationship problems — not isolated disputes. Prospective franchisees should call former franchisees listed in Item 20 and ask specifically about litigation experience before committing capital.

**Item 5 — Initial Fees**
- **What it contains**: All fees paid to the franchisor before opening, including the initial franchise fee, training fees, and any other pre-opening payments.
- **What to look for**: Initial franchise fees range from $10,000 (Chick-fil-A) to $50,000+ (McDonald's: $45,000; 7-Eleven: $25,000–$1M depending on store type). High initial fees are not inherently problematic if the AUV justifies the investment; low initial fees (Chick-fil-A) may indicate that the franchisor captures value through other mechanisms (profit sharing, required purchases).
- **Business Implication**: The initial franchise fee is the least important economic variable in franchise evaluation — it is a one-time cost that is dwarfed by the ongoing royalty obligation over a 20-year term. A $45,000 initial fee on a $1.5M AUV franchise generating $75,000/year in royalties represents less than 8 months of royalty payments. Franchisees who negotiate hard on the initial fee while ignoring the royalty rate are optimising the wrong variable.

**Item 6 — Other Fees**
- **What it contains**: All ongoing fees paid to the franchisor after opening, including royalties, advertising fund contributions, technology fees, training fees, and renewal fees.
- **What to look for**: Total ongoing fee burden (royalty + advertising fund + technology fee) is the critical metric. Fee burdens above 12–15% of gross sales significantly compress franchisee operating margins. Technology fees — increasingly common as franchisors deploy proprietary POS, ordering, and loyalty systems — can add $500–$2,000/month in fixed costs that are not reflected in the royalty percentage.
- **Business Implication**: A franchisee should model the total fee burden as a percentage of projected AUV — not as individual line items. A system with 6% royalty + 4% ad fund + $1,500/month technology fee on a $600,000 AUV unit has a total fee burden of 13% ($78,000/year) — equivalent to a 13% royalty with no technology fee. The technology fee's fixed nature makes it disproportionately burdensome for lower-AUV units.

**Item 7 — Estimated Initial Investment**
- **What it contains**: A table showing the estimated total investment required to open a franchise unit, including the initial franchise fee, real estate/leasehold improvements, equipment, initial inventory, working capital, and other pre-opening costs.
- **What to look for**: The range between low and high estimates is critical — a franchise with a $500,000–$2.5M investment range has significant uncertainty in capital requirements. Working capital estimates are frequently understated; experienced franchise attorneys recommend adding 20–30% to the franchisor's working capital estimate to account for the ramp-up period before the business reaches breakeven.
- **Business Implication**: A franchisee who funds their investment at the low end of the Item 7 range and encounters construction cost overruns or a slow ramp-up period will face a working capital crisis within 6–12 months of opening. Franchisees should plan for the high end of the investment range and maintain a 6-month operating expense reserve before opening.

**Item 12 — Territory**
- **What it contains**: The geographic territory granted to the franchisee, including any exclusivity provisions, the franchisor's right to operate competing units within or adjacent to the territory, and any restrictions on the franchisee's ability to solicit customers outside the territory.
- **What to look for**: "Protected territory" vs. "exclusive territory" is a critical distinction. A protected territory prevents the franchisor from opening company-owned or franchised units within the territory; an exclusive territory may also prevent the franchisor from selling through alternative channels (e-commerce, delivery platforms) within the territory. Many franchise agreements include "carve-outs" that allow the franchisor to sell through non-traditional channels (airports, stadiums, online) within the franchisee's territory without compensation.
- **Business Implication**: A franchisee in a dense urban market should insist on territory language that explicitly covers all digital and delivery channels — not just physical locations. A territory provision that protects against physical unit encroachment but allows the franchisor to operate ghost kitchens or delivery-only units within the territory provides no protection against the most significant competitive threat in modern QSR.

**Item 19 — Financial Performance Representations (FPR)**
- **What it contains**: Optional but increasingly common disclosure of the financial performance of existing franchise units, including average unit volumes (AUV), median gross sales, and in some cases, operating profit data.
- **What to look for**: Item 19 is the most important item for evaluating franchise economics. Franchisors are not required to provide Item 19 data, but approximately 65% of franchisors now include some form of FPR (IFA, 2024). When evaluating Item 19 data: (1) Distinguish between gross sales and net profit — AUV data shows revenue, not profitability; (2) Identify whether the data includes all units or only top performers; (3) Compare the median AUV (not the average, which is skewed by top performers) to the total investment required to calculate payback period.
- **Business Implication**: A franchisor who does not provide Item 19 data is withholding the most important information a prospective franchisee needs to evaluate the investment. Franchisees should treat the absence of Item 19 as a red flag and should call existing franchisees directly (using Item 20 contact information) to obtain financial performance data before signing.

**Item 20 — Outlets and Franchisee Information**
- **What it contains**: A table showing the number of franchise units opened, closed, transferred, and terminated in each of the past 3 years, plus contact information for all current and former franchisees.
- **What to look for**: High closure or termination rates are the most important red flag in the FDD. A franchise system that opened 100 units and closed 40 in the past 3 years has a 40% failure rate — a fact that is disclosed in Item 20 but not prominently highlighted. Calling former franchisees (whose contact information is required in Item 20) is the most valuable due diligence a prospective franchisee can conduct.
- **Business Implication**: A prospective franchisee should call at least 10 current and 5 former franchisees before signing — not to validate the franchisor's marketing claims, but to understand the real economics, the quality of franchisor support, and the reasons why former franchisees exited. Former franchisees who were terminated or chose not to renew provide the most candid assessments of system quality.

**Item 21 — Financial Statements**
- **What it contains**: Audited financial statements for the franchisor for the past 3 fiscal years, including balance sheet, income statement, and cash flow statement.
- **What to look for**: Franchisor financial health is critical — a financially distressed franchisor may reduce support services, sell the brand to a private equity firm with different priorities, or go bankrupt (leaving franchisees without brand support). Franchisors with declining royalty revenue (indicating shrinking system sales) or negative cash flow from operations are high-risk investments.
- **Business Implication**: A franchisor whose royalty revenue has declined for 2+ consecutive years is operating a shrinking system — a signal that existing franchisees are closing faster than new ones are opening. A franchisee who invests $1.5M in a shrinking system faces the risk of brand deterioration, reduced marketing support, and a declining resale value for their franchise at the end of the term.

| FDD Item | Content | Primary Risk Indicator |

> **Key Takeaway**: Item 19 (Financial Performance Representations) and Item 20 (unit closure/termination data) are the two most important FDD items for evaluating franchise investment risk. A franchise system with a median AUV of $500,000 and a 3-year closure rate of 15% is a fundamentally different investment than one with a median AUV of $1.5M and a 3-year closure rate of 3% — a distinction that is only visible in the FDD.

## 4. Franchise Economics

### 4.1 Fee Structures and Revenue Model

**Initial Franchise Fee**
- **Definition**: A one-time, non-refundable payment made by the franchisee to the franchisor upon signing the Franchise Agreement, granting the franchisee the right to use the franchisor's brand, systems, and intellectual property for the term of the agreement.
- **Mechanics**: Initial franchise fees range from $10,000 (Chick-fil-A) to $50,000+ (McDonald's: $45,000; Dunkin': $40,000–$90,000 depending on location type; 7-Eleven: $25,000–$1M for existing stores). The initial fee is typically non-refundable even if the franchisee fails to open — it compensates the franchisor for the cost of franchisee recruitment, training, and pre-opening support. Initial fees are a relatively small component of total investment (typically 2–10% of total initial investment) but are the first financial commitment in the franchise relationship.
- **Example**: Dunkin's initial franchise fee of $40,000–$90,000 (depending on location type and development agreement) is paid before the franchisee has opened a single store. Dunkin's total initial investment of $437,500–$1,787,500 per location means the initial fee represents approximately 5–9% of total investment. Dunkin's 13,000+ US locations generate approximately $520M–$1.17B in initial franchise fees over the life of the system — a significant revenue stream for the franchisor that is recognised upfront (Dunkin' FDD, 2023; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A prospective franchisee should treat the initial franchise fee as a sunk cost from the moment it is paid — it is non-refundable regardless of whether the business succeeds. The relevant financial question is not "is the initial fee reasonable?" but "does the projected AUV and operating margin generate a payback on total initial investment (including the fee) within 5–7 years?" A $45,000 McDonald's franchise fee is trivial relative to a $1.65M total investment; a $45,000 fee on a $150,000 total investment represents 30% of capital at risk before the doors open.

**Royalty Structure**
- **Definition**: The ongoing fee paid by the franchisee to the franchisor as a percentage of gross sales (or, less commonly, a fixed weekly/monthly fee), representing the primary ongoing revenue stream for the franchisor and the primary ongoing cost for the franchisee.
- **Mechanics**: Royalty rates in major franchise systems range from 4% (McDonald's) to 15% (Chick-fil-A) of gross sales. The royalty is typically calculated on gross sales (before any deductions for food cost, labour, or other expenses), making it a fixed percentage of revenue regardless of franchisee profitability. Some franchise systems use a **sliding scale royalty** — a lower rate for lower-volume units and a higher rate for higher-volume units — to balance franchisee economics across the system. The royalty is the franchisor's primary profit driver: McDonald's collects approximately $4.5B annually in royalties on $100B+ in system-wide sales (4% × $112B = $4.5B) (McDonald's Annual Report, 2023).

```
Royalty Calculation:
  Gross Sales:          $1,500,000 (annual unit revenue)
  Royalty Rate:         5%
  Annual Royalty:       $75,000
  Advertising Fund:     4% = $60,000
  Technology Fee:       $500/month = $6,000
  Total Annual Fees:    $141,000 (9.4% of gross sales)
  
  Franchisee P&L (QSR example):
    Gross Sales:        $1,500,000   100%
    Food Cost:          $450,000      30%
    Labour:             $375,000      25%
    Occupancy:          $150,000      10%
    Total Fees:         $141,000       9.4%
    Other Operating:    $75,000        5%
    Operating Profit:   $309,000      20.6%
```

- **Example**: Subway's royalty structure — 8% of gross sales + 4.5% advertising fund = 12.5% total fee burden — is among the highest in QSR. On Subway's AUV of approximately $450,000, this generates $56,250 in annual fees to Subway. After food cost (approximately 30% = $135,000), labour (approximately 30% = $135,000), and occupancy (approximately 12% = $54,000), a Subway franchisee's operating profit is approximately $69,750 (15.5% of sales) — a thin margin that explains Subway's chronic franchisee dissatisfaction and the closure of approximately 7,000 US locations between 2015 and 2022 (Subway FDD, 2023; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A franchisor setting royalty rates must balance two competing interests: maximising royalty income (which favours higher rates) and maximising franchisee profitability (which favours lower rates). Franchisors who set royalties above 6–7% of gross sales on systems with AUVs below $750,000 systematically create franchisee financial distress — which leads to brand standard violations, closures, and litigation that ultimately destroys more franchisor value than the incremental royalty income generates.

**Advertising Fund Contribution**
- **Definition**: A mandatory ongoing fee (typically 2–5% of gross sales) paid by franchisees into a pooled advertising fund controlled by the franchisor, used to fund national and regional marketing campaigns, brand advertising, and promotional programmes.
- **Mechanics**: Advertising fund contributions are separate from royalties and are legally required to be spent on advertising and marketing (not on franchisor operations). Franchisors are required to provide annual accounting of advertising fund expenditures. The advertising fund creates a collective action problem: individual franchisees benefit from national advertising but have limited control over how the fund is spent. Franchisee associations (e.g., the National Franchisee Association for McDonald's) negotiate with franchisors over advertising fund allocation — a source of ongoing tension in many franchise systems.
- **Example**: McDonald's advertising fund — funded by 4% of gross sales from all franchisees — generates approximately $4.5B annually, making it the largest restaurant advertising budget in the world. McDonald's advertising fund pays for national TV campaigns, digital advertising, the MyMcDonald's app, and promotional programmes (McRib, Monopoly game). McDonald's franchisees have historically contested advertising fund allocations — particularly the shift from TV advertising to digital/app investment — arguing that digital advertising benefits McDonald's Corporation (through data collection) more than individual franchisees (McDonald's Annual Report, 2023; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A franchisee evaluating a franchise system should request the past 3 years of advertising fund financial statements (required to be provided under the FTC Franchise Rule) and verify that 100% of contributions are spent on advertising — not on franchisor overhead. Franchisees in small markets should assess whether national advertising campaigns (which skew toward large metro markets) generate proportional traffic lift in their specific location before committing to a high advertising fund contribution rate.

**Technology Fee**
- **Definition**: An ongoing fee charged by franchisors for proprietary technology systems (POS, ordering platforms, loyalty programmes, kitchen management systems) that franchisees are required to use, increasingly common as franchise systems invest in digital infrastructure.
- **Mechanics**: Technology fees range from $200–$2,000/month per location and are typically charged in addition to royalties and advertising fund contributions. Technology fees are a growing source of franchisee-franchisor tension: franchisees argue that they are paying for technology that primarily benefits the franchisor (data collection, brand control) rather than the franchisee. Franchisors counter that proprietary technology creates competitive advantage and operational consistency that benefits the entire system.
- **Example**: 7-Eleven charges franchisees a technology fee of approximately $1,500–$2,000/month for its proprietary retail information system (RIS), which manages inventory, ordering, and financial reporting. On a 7-Eleven AUV of approximately $1.5M, this represents approximately 1.2–1.6% of gross sales in technology fees — in addition to the 34–52% of gross profit that 7-Eleven retains under its unique profit-sharing royalty model (7-Eleven FDD, 2023; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A franchisee should model technology fees as a fixed cost (not a percentage of sales) when evaluating franchise economics — a $2,000/month technology fee represents 4.8% of gross sales on a $500,000 AUV unit but only 1.6% on a $1.5M AUV unit. Franchisors who charge technology fees for systems that primarily benefit the franchisor (data collection, centralised reporting) rather than the franchisee (customer-facing ordering, loyalty) should expect franchisee resistance and should be prepared to demonstrate the ROI of the technology investment at the unit level.

> **Key Takeaway**: Total franchise fee burden — royalty + advertising fund + technology fee — is the single most important economic variable in franchise evaluation. A 1-percentage-point difference in royalty rate on a $1M AUV franchise generates $10,000 in annual franchisee operating profit difference. Franchisees who focus only on the royalty rate and ignore advertising fund and technology fees systematically underestimate their total fee obligation.

## 5. Franchise Operations

### 5.1 Brand Standards and Operational Playbooks

**Brand Standards**
- **Definition**: The documented specifications for every customer-facing element of the franchise operation — store design, signage, uniforms, menu/product offerings, service protocols, and marketing materials — that franchisees are contractually required to follow to maintain brand consistency across the network.
- **Mechanics**: Brand standards are enforced through the **Operations Manual** — a proprietary, confidential document (typically 200–500 pages) that specifies every operational procedure in detail. The Operations Manual is not provided to prospective franchisees before signing (it is proprietary), but the FDD must describe its general contents. Brand standards cover: (1) **Physical standards** — store layout, equipment specifications, signage dimensions, colour codes; (2) **Product standards** — approved suppliers, recipe specifications, portion sizes, quality parameters; (3) **Service standards** — greeting scripts, service time targets, complaint resolution procedures; (4) **Marketing standards** — approved promotional materials, social media guidelines, local advertising requirements.
- **Example**: McDonald's Operations Manual — approximately 600 pages — specifies that a Quarter Pounder patty must weigh exactly 4 oz pre-cook and be cooked to an internal temperature of 160°F, that french fries must be discarded after 7 minutes in the holding bin, and that the drive-through greeting must be delivered within 30 seconds of a car arriving at the speaker. These specifications are not suggestions — franchisees who consistently fail to meet them face remediation plans and potential franchise termination. McDonald's brand standards are the foundation of its global consistency: a McDonald's Big Mac in Tokyo tastes identical to one in Chicago because the Operations Manual specifies every ingredient, preparation step, and quality check (McDonald's Operations Manual summary; approximate benchmark (2023); see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A franchisee who views brand standards as optional rather than contractual obligations is taking a significant legal and financial risk. Franchise agreements typically include termination clauses for "material breach" of brand standards — a franchisor can terminate a franchise agreement (and the franchisee's $500,000–$2.5M investment) for repeated brand standard violations. Franchisees who invest in training and compliance infrastructure to meet brand standards consistently outperform those who cut corners.

**Operational Playbook**
- **Definition**: The comprehensive, step-by-step guide to running a franchise unit, encompassing opening and closing procedures, staffing models, inventory management, equipment maintenance, food safety protocols, and customer service standards; the operational implementation of the brand standards.
- **Mechanics**: Operational playbooks are updated continuously as the franchisor refines best practices. Franchisors communicate playbook updates through field operations consultants, franchisee intranets, and mandatory training programmes. The playbook is the primary tool for ensuring operational consistency across a franchise network — a franchisee in Phoenix and a franchisee in Boston should deliver an identical customer experience because they are following the same playbook.
- **Example**: Chick-fil-A's operational playbook — developed over 70+ years of company-owned operations before franchising — is widely regarded as the most detailed and effective in QSR. Chick-fil-A's playbook specifies the "Second Mile Service" standard: every customer interaction should exceed expectations, not just meet them. Chick-fil-A's drive-through operators are trained to say "My pleasure" (not "You're welcome") in response to customer thanks — a brand standard that has become a cultural identifier. Chick-fil-A's AUV of $8.7M — 2.4x McDonald's $3.6M AUV — is partially attributable to the operational excellence embedded in its playbook (Chick-fil-A investor presentations; approximate benchmark (2023); see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A franchisor who launches a franchise system before the operational playbook is fully documented is setting franchisees up to fail. The playbook must be detailed enough that a franchisee with no prior industry experience can open and operate a unit to brand standards — if it requires the franchisor to be physically present to train every new franchisee, the system is not ready to scale. Franchisors should pilot the playbook in 3–5 company-owned locations before franchising to identify gaps.

### 5.2 Compliance Monitoring

**Franchise Compliance Programme**
- **Definition**: The systematic process by which a franchisor monitors, evaluates, and enforces franchisee adherence to brand standards, operational procedures, and contractual obligations, using field operations consultants, mystery shopping, technology monitoring, and formal audit programmes.
- **Mechanics**: Compliance monitoring operates at three levels: (1) **Continuous monitoring** — POS data, kitchen display system data, and drive-through timer data are monitored centrally in real time, flagging deviations from standards (e.g., service times exceeding targets, menu items sold outside approved hours); (2) **Periodic audits** — field operations consultants conduct quarterly or semi-annual operational reviews, scoring franchisees against a standardised checklist; (3) **Mystery shopping** — third-party evaluators conduct unannounced visits to assess service quality, food quality, and cleanliness. Franchisees who fail compliance audits receive remediation plans with specific improvement targets and timelines; repeated failures can trigger franchise agreement termination.
- **Example**: McDonald's field operations structure — approximately 1 field consultant per 20–25 franchisee locations — means each consultant manages approximately $72–90M in annual system sales. McDonald's consultants conduct quarterly Business Reviews with each franchisee, reviewing operational metrics (speed of service, order accuracy, cleanliness scores), financial performance (AUV vs. system average), and compliance with brand standards. McDonald's mystery shopping programme — conducted by a third-party firm across all 40,000+ global locations — generates a monthly "ROIP" (Restaurant Operations Improvement Process) score that is used to rank franchisees and identify underperformers (McDonald's Annual Report, 2023; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A franchisor who relies solely on annual audits for compliance monitoring will discover brand standard violations months after they begin — by which time customer perception damage has already occurred. Real-time technology monitoring (POS data, drive-through timers, kitchen display systems) enables franchisors to identify and address compliance issues within 24–48 hours, before they become systemic. The cost of compliance technology ($500–$1,000/location/year) is recovered within months through the brand equity protection it provides.

> **Key Takeaway**: Franchise compliance is the primary mechanism for protecting brand equity across a distributed network. A single franchisee who consistently delivers substandard food quality or service can damage the brand perception of all 40,000 McDonald's locations — because customers generalise from individual experiences to the brand. Franchisors that invest in robust compliance infrastructure (field consultants, technology monitoring, mystery shopping) protect the brand equity that is the foundation of the entire franchise system's value.

## 6. Franchise Support

### 6.1 Training Programmes

**Initial Training Programme**
- **Definition**: The mandatory pre-opening training provided by the franchisor to new franchisees and their key management staff, covering brand standards, operational procedures, financial management, and customer service, typically lasting 2–12 weeks at a franchisor training centre and/or an existing franchise location.
- **Mechanics**: Initial training is the franchisee's primary introduction to the franchise system. Training programmes range from 2 weeks (simple service concepts) to 12+ weeks (complex operations like McDonald's "Hamburger University"). Training covers: (1) **Operational training** — hands-on practice in all station positions (grill, fryer, counter, drive-through); (2) **Management training** — scheduling, inventory management, financial reporting, HR compliance; (3) **Brand training** — brand history, values, customer service standards; (4) **Technology training** — POS system, ordering platform, reporting tools. Franchisees typically pay for their own travel and accommodation during training; some franchisors charge a training fee ($1,000–$5,000) in addition to the initial franchise fee.
- **Example**: McDonald's "Hamburger University" — established 1961 in Oak Brook, Illinois — has trained approximately 275,000 franchise operators and managers since its founding. McDonald's training programme for new franchisees is approximately 9 months, combining classroom instruction at Hamburger University with hands-on training at existing McDonald's locations. McDonald's training investment per franchisee is estimated at $15,000–$25,000 — a cost borne by McDonald's as part of its franchise support commitment. McDonald's training programme is widely regarded as the gold standard in franchise training, and its alumni include executives at major restaurant chains worldwide (McDonald's Annual Report, 2023; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A prospective franchisee should evaluate training programme quality as a leading indicator of franchise system quality — franchisors who invest $15,000–$25,000 per franchisee in training are demonstrating commitment to franchisee success; franchisors who provide 2-week training programmes for complex operations are signalling that franchisee support is not a priority. Training quality correlates directly with franchisee satisfaction scores and AUV performance across franchise systems (Franchise Business Review, 2024).

### 6.2 Site Selection Assistance

**Site Selection Support**
- **Definition**: The franchisor's assistance to franchisees in identifying, evaluating, and securing real estate locations that meet the brand's site criteria (traffic counts, demographics, visibility, co-tenancy, parking), reducing the risk of opening in a suboptimal location.
- **Mechanics**: Site selection support ranges from basic criteria guidance (minimum traffic counts, demographic requirements) to full real estate services (site identification, lease negotiation, construction management). Franchisors with real estate expertise (McDonald's, 7-Eleven) own or control the real estate and lease it to franchisees — giving the franchisor significant leverage over site quality and franchisee compliance. Franchisors without real estate capabilities provide site approval processes: the franchisee identifies a site, and the franchisor approves or rejects it based on brand criteria.
- **Example**: 7-Eleven's site selection process — managed by 7-Eleven's real estate team — identifies and secures locations before offering them to franchisees. 7-Eleven's real estate team evaluates 50,000+ potential sites annually, approving approximately 500–700 new US locations per year. 7-Eleven's site selection criteria include minimum daily traffic counts of 10,000+ vehicles, proximity to residential density of 5,000+ households within 1 mile, and co-tenancy with complementary businesses (gas stations, fast food). 7-Eleven's real estate control — it owns or controls the lease for approximately 80% of its US locations — gives it significant leverage over franchisee compliance and location quality (7-Eleven FDD, 2023; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A franchisee who selects their own site without franchisor approval is taking the single largest risk in the franchise investment — location quality is the primary determinant of AUV, and a poor location cannot be overcome by operational excellence. Franchisees should insist on franchisor site approval (not just site criteria guidance) and should independently verify traffic counts, demographic data, and competitive density before committing to a lease.

### 6.3 Marketing Support

**National Advertising Fund**
- **Definition**: The pooled advertising fund (funded by franchisee contributions of 2–5% of gross sales) used by the franchisor to execute national brand advertising campaigns, digital marketing, promotional programmes, and new product launches that benefit the entire franchise system.
- **Mechanics**: National advertising fund management is a significant source of franchisee-franchisor tension. Franchisees contribute mandatory percentages of gross sales but have limited control over how the funds are spent. Franchisors are required to provide annual accounting of fund expenditures but are not required to spend the funds in proportion to each franchisee's contribution (i.e., a franchisee in a small market may contribute to national TV advertising that primarily benefits franchisees in large markets). Franchisee associations negotiate with franchisors over fund allocation — particularly the balance between national brand advertising and local market support.
- **Example**: Subway's advertising fund — funded by 4.5% of gross sales from 37,000+ locations — generates approximately $750M annually. Subway's advertising fund management has been a persistent source of franchisee complaints: franchisees have alleged that Subway's advertising fund was used to fund corporate overhead rather than advertising, and that national campaigns (featuring celebrity endorsers like Jared Fogle) damaged the brand rather than supporting it. Subway's 2021 brand refresh — new logo, menu redesign, "Eat Fresh Refresh" campaign — was funded by the advertising fund and generated a 7% increase in same-store sales in 2021, demonstrating the potential of well-executed national advertising (Subway investor presentations; approximate benchmark (2023); see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A franchisee in a system with a 4–5% advertising fund contribution should expect national campaigns to generate measurable traffic lift — if SSSG is flat or negative despite significant advertising fund investment, the fund is either being misallocated or the brand has a positioning problem that advertising cannot solve. Franchisees should participate in franchisee advisory councils to influence advertising fund allocation toward campaigns with demonstrated ROI.

> **Key Takeaway**: Franchise support quality — training, site selection, marketing — is the primary differentiator between franchise systems that generate strong franchisee returns and those that generate chronic franchisee dissatisfaction. Franchisors that invest in support infrastructure (Hamburger University, real estate teams, national advertising) generate higher AUVs, lower closure rates, and stronger franchisee satisfaction scores — all of which drive franchise system value.

## 7. Franchise Performance Management

### 7.1 Benchmarking and Performance Metrics

**Average Unit Volume (AUV)**
- **Definition**: The average annual gross sales revenue generated by a single franchise unit within a system, the primary benchmark for evaluating franchise system performance and individual unit performance relative to system peers.
- **Mechanics**: AUV is the most widely cited franchise performance metric because it directly determines franchisee economics: higher AUV means more gross profit to cover fixed costs (royalties, rent, labour) and generate franchisee operating income. AUV varies significantly across franchise systems: McDonald's $3.6M AUV vs. Subway's $450,000 AUV reflects the fundamental difference in their business models (full-service QSR vs. fast casual sandwich). AUV also varies within a system by location type (urban vs. suburban), market size, and franchisee operational quality.

```
AUV-Based Franchisee Economics:
  System A: AUV $1,500,000, Royalty 5%, Ad Fund 4%, Total Fees 9%
    Annual Fees: $135,000
    Food Cost (30%): $450,000
    Labour (28%): $420,000
    Occupancy (10%): $150,000
    Other (5%): $75,000
    Operating Profit: $270,000 (18% margin)
    
  System B: AUV $500,000, Royalty 8%, Ad Fund 4.5%, Total Fees 12.5%
    Annual Fees: $62,500
    Food Cost (30%): $150,000
    Labour (30%): $150,000
    Occupancy (12%): $60,000
    Other (5%): $25,000
    Operating Profit: $52,500 (10.5% margin)
```

- **Example**: Chick-fil-A's AUV of $8.7M (2023) — the highest in QSR — is 2.4x McDonald's $3.6M and 19x Subway's $450,000. Chick-fil-A's AUV advantage is driven by: (1) Highly selective franchisee selection (fewer than 1% acceptance rate); (2) Operator-only model (each franchisee operates a single location, ensuring full management attention); (3) Closed on Sundays (concentrating demand into 6 days); (4) Drive-through efficiency (face-to-face ordering model). Chick-fil-A's AUV generates approximately $1.3M in annual operating profit per franchisee (at 15% operating margin) — the highest franchisee earnings in QSR (Chick-fil-A investor presentations; approximate benchmark (2023); see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A franchisee whose AUV is below 80% of the system average should treat this as an urgent operational signal — not a market condition to accept. The gap between a $450,000 AUV unit and a $600,000 AUV unit in the same system (same royalty rate, same occupancy cost) generates approximately $30,000–$50,000 in additional annual operating profit — a difference that compounds over a 20-year franchise term into $600,000–$1M in cumulative earnings. AUV improvement is the highest-ROI operational focus for any underperforming franchisee.

**Same-Store Sales Growth (SSSG)**
- **Definition**: The year-over-year percentage change in gross sales for franchise units that have been open for at least 12 months (or the franchisor's defined comparable period), measuring organic revenue growth independent of new unit openings.
- **Mechanics**: SSSG is the primary indicator of franchise system health. Positive SSSG indicates that existing units are growing revenue — driven by traffic growth, average check growth, or both. Negative SSSG indicates that existing units are losing revenue — a warning sign of brand deterioration, competitive pressure, or operational decline. Franchisors report SSSG in their annual reports and FDDs; franchisees use SSSG to benchmark their individual performance against the system average.
- **Example**: McDonald's US SSSG of 8.7% in 2023 — driven by menu price increases of approximately 10% and digital ordering growth — reflects the strength of its brand and operational improvements. McDonald's SSSG has been positive for 14 consecutive years (2010–2023), demonstrating the durability of its franchise system. In contrast, Subway's US SSSG was negative for multiple years between 2015 and 2020 — a period during which Subway closed approximately 7,000 US locations — before recovering to +7% in 2021 following its brand refresh (McDonald's Annual Report, 2023; Subway investor presentations, 2023).
- **Business Implication**: A franchisee whose SSSG is negative while the system average is positive is losing competitive ground within the network — and is at elevated risk of non-renewal at the end of their franchise term. Franchisees should benchmark their SSSG against the system average quarterly and investigate the root cause of any underperformance (operational issues, local competition, demographic shifts) before it becomes a compliance issue with the franchisor.

### 7.2 Underperforming Unit Management

**Underperforming Franchisee Management**
- **Definition**: The structured process by which a franchisor identifies, supports, and — if necessary — terminates franchise agreements with franchisees whose unit performance falls below system standards, balancing the franchisee's investment protection with the brand's need for consistent performance.
- **Mechanics**: Underperforming unit management follows a defined escalation process: (1) **Performance identification** — units with AUV below 80% of system average, declining SSSG, or compliance audit failures are flagged; (2) **Remediation plan** — the franchisee and field consultant develop a 90–180 day improvement plan with specific targets; (3) **Monitoring** — weekly check-ins and monthly performance reviews during the remediation period; (4) **Escalation** — if targets are not met, the franchisor may issue a "Notice of Default" under the Franchise Agreement; (5) **Termination** — if the default is not cured within the specified period (typically 30–60 days), the franchisor may terminate the franchise agreement. Termination is a last resort — the cost of litigation, reputational damage, and finding a replacement franchisee makes termination expensive for both parties.
- **Example**: Subway's closure of approximately 7,000 US locations between 2015 and 2022 — from 27,000 to 20,000 US units — was the largest franchise network contraction in QSR history. Subway's approach combined voluntary closures (franchisees who chose not to renew), negotiated buyouts, and forced terminations for franchisees who failed to meet brand standards. Subway's "Fresh Forward" remodel programme — requiring franchisees to invest $50,000–$100,000 in store upgrades — accelerated closures among franchisees who could not afford the investment. The network contraction, while painful, improved Subway's average unit economics: AUV increased from approximately $420,000 (2015) to approximately $450,000 (2023) as underperforming units were removed from the system (Subway investor presentations; approximate benchmark (2023); see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A franchisor who measures system health by unit count rather than AUV and franchisee profitability will systematically over-expand into markets that cannot support the brand's economics — creating the conditions for the kind of mass closure that damaged Subway's brand between 2015 and 2022. The correct metric for franchise system health is the percentage of franchisees generating operating margins above 15% — not the total number of locations.

> **Key Takeaway**: Underperforming unit management is the most difficult operational challenge in franchise management — it requires balancing the franchisee's legal rights (franchise agreements are contracts) with the brand's need for consistent performance. Franchisors that allow underperforming units to persist damage the brand for all franchisees; franchisors that terminate aggressively face litigation and reputational damage. The optimal approach is early intervention with structured remediation plans before performance deteriorates to the point where termination is the only option.

## 8. Franchise Legal Considerations

### 8.1 Territory Rights, Termination, and Renewal

**Territory Rights**
- **Definition**: The geographic area within which a franchisee has the exclusive or protected right to operate franchise units, defined in the Franchise Agreement and the FDD, representing one of the most commercially significant provisions in the franchise relationship.
- **Mechanics**: Territory rights exist on a spectrum: (1) **Exclusive territory** — no other franchisee or company-owned unit may operate within the defined area; (2) **Protected territory** — the franchisor will not open competing units within the area, but may sell through alternative channels (online, delivery platforms, non-traditional venues); (3) **No territory** — the franchisee has no geographic protection (common in home-based service franchises). Territory size is negotiated based on population density, market potential, and the franchisee's development commitment. **Encroachment** — the franchisor opening a competing unit within or adjacent to a franchisee's territory — is one of the most common sources of franchisee-franchisor litigation.
- **Example**: Domino's Pizza's territory system — each franchisee receives an exclusive territory based on a defined number of households (typically 8,000–12,000 households per unit) — has been the subject of significant litigation. Domino's franchisees in dense urban markets have sued Domino's for encroachment when Domino's opened delivery-only "ghost kitchen" units within their exclusive territories, arguing that ghost kitchens compete directly with their delivery business. Domino's has argued that ghost kitchens are a different "channel" not covered by the exclusive territory provision — a legal distinction that courts have ruled on inconsistently (Domino's Annual Report, 2023; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A franchisee negotiating territory rights should insist on language that explicitly covers all channels through which the franchisor may generate sales within the territory — including delivery platforms, ghost kitchens, e-commerce, and non-traditional venues. Territory provisions written in 2005 that do not address digital channels are legally ambiguous in 2025 — and the cost of litigating that ambiguity (typically $200,000–$500,000 in legal fees) falls on the franchisee, not the franchisor.

**Termination Clauses**
- **Definition**: The provisions in the Franchise Agreement specifying the conditions under which the franchisor may terminate the franchise relationship before the end of the agreement term, including both "with cause" termination (for franchisee default) and, in some agreements, "without cause" termination (with compensation).
- **Mechanics**: Franchise agreements distinguish between two types of termination: (1) **Termination with cause** — the franchisee has materially breached the agreement (failure to pay royalties, repeated brand standard violations, criminal conviction, bankruptcy); the franchisor must provide notice and a cure period (typically 30–60 days for curable defaults); (2) **Termination without cause** — rare in US franchise agreements but common in some international markets; requires the franchisor to pay compensation to the franchisee. Post-termination obligations are critical: most franchise agreements include non-compete clauses (typically 2 years within a defined radius of the former franchise location) and require the franchisee to de-identify the location (remove all brand signage, return proprietary materials).
- **Example**: 7-Eleven's franchise agreement includes a provision allowing 7-Eleven to terminate the franchise agreement if the franchisee's gross profit falls below a minimum threshold for two consecutive years — a "financial performance" termination trigger that is unusual in franchise agreements. This provision has been the subject of franchisee litigation, with franchisees arguing that 7-Eleven's pricing and product requirements (which affect gross profit) make it impossible to meet the minimum threshold in certain markets. 7-Eleven settled a class action lawsuit with approximately 1,000 franchisees in 2019 for $152M, related to alleged violations of California labour law and franchise agreement terms (7-Eleven FDD, 2023; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A franchisee reviewing termination clauses should pay particular attention to "financial performance" termination triggers — provisions that allow the franchisor to terminate based on sales or profit thresholds that the franchisee may not be able to control (e.g., if the franchisor's pricing or product requirements directly affect the metric). These provisions should be negotiated to include a "cure period" and to exclude performance shortfalls caused by franchisor actions before they are signed.

**Renewal Terms**
- **Definition**: The provisions in the Franchise Agreement specifying the franchisee's right to renew the agreement at the end of the initial term, including the conditions for renewal, the renewal fee, and whether the renewal will be on the same terms as the original agreement or on the franchisor's then-current standard terms.
- **Mechanics**: Franchise agreement terms typically run 10–20 years, with renewal options of 5–10 years. Renewal is not automatic — franchisees must meet performance standards, complete required remodels, and sign the franchisor's then-current Franchise Agreement (which may have materially different terms than the original). The "then-current terms" renewal provision is a significant risk for franchisees: a franchisee who signed a 20-year agreement in 2005 may face significantly higher royalties, more restrictive territory provisions, or mandatory technology investments when renewing in 2025. Franchisees who have invested $500,000–$2.5M in a location have significant leverage to negotiate renewal terms — but only if they have maintained strong performance and compliance.
- **Example**: McDonald's franchise agreement renewal — McDonald's requires franchisees to complete a full restaurant remodel (estimated cost: $300,000–$700,000) as a condition of renewal. McDonald's "Experience of the Future" (EOTF) remodel programme — self-ordering kiosks, table service, digital menu boards, modernised décor — was required for all US franchise renewals between 2018 and 2022. Franchisees who could not afford the remodel investment were not renewed, resulting in approximately 500–700 US location closures during the EOTF rollout period. McDonald's justified the requirement by demonstrating that EOTF-remodelled locations generated 5–8% higher SSSG than non-remodelled locations (McDonald's Annual Report, 2023; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A franchisee approaching the end of a 20-year franchise term should begin renewal negotiations 3–5 years before expiry — not 6 months before. The franchisor's leverage is highest at renewal (the franchisee has a fully invested location with no alternative use); the franchisee's leverage is highest when they have 3–5 years of strong performance and compliance to demonstrate. Franchisees who wait until the final year to negotiate renewal terms will face the franchisor's then-current standard agreement with minimal room to negotiate.

> **Key Takeaway**: Franchise legal provisions — territory rights, termination clauses, and renewal terms — are the contractual framework that determines the long-term economics of the franchise investment. A franchisee who signs a 20-year agreement without understanding the renewal terms (particularly the "then-current terms" provision) may face a materially different economic arrangement at renewal. Franchise attorneys who specialise in franchise law are essential advisors for both franchisors and franchisees navigating these provisions.

## 9. Wholesale / Cash & Carry Models

### 9.1 Wholesale and Cash & Carry Fundamentals

**Wholesale / Cash & Carry**
- **Definition**: A retail format that sells products in bulk quantities at prices below standard retail, exclusively to registered business customers (restaurants, retailers, caterers, small businesses) who pay cash (or card) and transport purchases themselves, eliminating the credit and delivery costs of traditional wholesale distribution.
- **Mechanics**: Cash & carry operations differ from consumer retail in three fundamental ways: (1) **Customer qualification** — only registered businesses with a valid trade account (VAT number, business licence) may shop; (2) **Pack sizes** — products are sold in bulk (case quantities, catering packs) rather than consumer units; (3) **Pricing** — trade prices are 15–30% below consumer retail prices, reflecting the elimination of delivery cost, credit risk, and the customer's self-service model. Cash & carry operators generate gross margins of 10–18% vs. 25–35% for consumer supermarkets — lower margins compensated by lower operating costs (no delivery fleet, no credit management, lower labour per unit sold).
- **Example**: Metro AG (Germany) — the world's largest cash & carry operator with €29.8B in annual revenue across 680+ stores in 35 countries — serves approximately 21 million registered business customers including restaurants, hotels, caterers, and independent retailers. Metro's average transaction value of approximately €200–500 (vs. €50–80 for consumer supermarkets) reflects the bulk purchasing behaviour of its trade customers. Metro's gross margin of approximately 16% is below consumer grocery (25–30%) but its operating cost structure (approximately 12% of sales) is also lower, generating an operating margin of approximately 3–4% (Metro AG Annual Report, 2024).
- **Business Implication**: A restaurant operator or independent retailer who sources exclusively from full-service distributors (Sysco, US Foods) without benchmarking prices against Metro, Makro, or Costco Business Center is systematically overpaying for commodity items. A hybrid sourcing strategy — full-service distributor for perishables and specialty items (where delivery frequency and credit terms justify the premium), cash & carry for dry goods and packaging (where price is the primary variable) — typically reduces total food and supply cost by 5–10% without sacrificing service quality.

**Costco Business Center**
- **Definition**: A Costco Wholesale format specifically designed for business customers, offering a broader range of foodservice, janitorial, and office supply products in larger pack sizes than standard Costco warehouses, with extended operating hours and delivery services for business customers.
- **Mechanics**: Costco Business Centers differ from standard Costco warehouses in three ways: (1) **Assortment** — 70% of SKUs are business-focused (foodservice packaging, commercial cleaning supplies, bulk food ingredients) vs. 30% consumer-focused; (2) **Pack sizes** — larger case quantities (e.g., 6-gallon containers of cooking oil vs. 1-gallon at standard Costco); (3) **Services** — next-day delivery for business customers within the delivery zone, eliminating the need for business customers to transport bulk purchases. Costco operates approximately 28 Business Centers in the US, generating estimated annual revenue of $3–4B — a small but strategically important segment of Costco's $242B total revenue (FY2024).
- **Example**: A restaurant operator purchasing from Costco Business Center vs. a traditional foodservice distributor (Sysco, US Foods) faces a trade-off: Costco Business Center prices are approximately 10–15% below Sysco/US Foods on comparable items, but Costco Business Center requires the operator to transport purchases (or pay for delivery), manage their own inventory, and purchase in larger quantities. A 50-seat restaurant spending $15,000/month on food purchases saves approximately $1,500–$2,250/month by sourcing from Costco Business Center — but must invest in storage capacity and transportation logistics to capture the savings (Costco Annual Report, 2024; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A retail buyer or category manager evaluating Costco Business Center as a sourcing channel should model the total cost of ownership — not just the purchase price. The $1,500–$2,250/month savings on a $15,000/month food spend is compelling, but requires the operator to invest in a vehicle (or delivery fee), additional dry storage, and the labour time to shop and transport. For operators with limited storage and no vehicle, the net savings after logistics cost may be $500–$800/month — still meaningful, but less transformative than the headline price difference suggests.

**Metro/Makro vs. Consumer Retail: Key Differences**

| Dimension | Metro/Makro (Cash & Carry) | Consumer Supermarket |
|---|---|---|
| **Customer** | Registered businesses only | General public |
| **Pack Size** | Case/bulk quantities | Consumer units |
| **Pricing** | Trade price (15–30% below retail) | Consumer retail price |
| **Payment** | Cash/card, no credit | Cash/card/credit |
| **Delivery** | Customer self-transport (or paid delivery) | Home delivery available |
| **Gross Margin** | 10–18% | 25–35% |
| **Average Transaction** | €200–500 | €50–80 |
| **Operating Hours** | Extended (often 24/7) | Standard retail hours |
| **Membership** | Trade account required | Open to all |

*Sources: Metro AG Annual Report 2024; Costco Annual Report 2024; IBISWorld Wholesale Trade Report 2024.*

> **Key Takeaway**: Cash & carry operators compete on price and convenience for business customers who prioritise cost over service. The elimination of delivery, credit, and consumer-format packaging reduces operating costs by 8–12 percentage points vs. consumer retail, enabling trade prices that are 15–30% below consumer retail while maintaining acceptable operating margins. Retailers and restaurateurs who use cash & carry as a supplementary sourcing channel (for top-up purchases, specialty items, or emergency stock) capture significant cost savings without the minimum order requirements of traditional wholesale distribution.

## 10. B2B E-commerce Platforms

### 10.1 B2B Digital Commerce

**B2B E-commerce**
- **Definition**: The digital sale of products and services between businesses — manufacturer to distributor, distributor to retailer, or supplier to business end-user — conducted through online platforms, EDI (Electronic Data Interchange), or API-based ordering systems, with the US B2B e-commerce market projected to reach $3.0 trillion by 2027 (Forrester, 2024).
- **Mechanics**: B2B e-commerce differs from B2C in five structural ways: (1) **Complex pricing** — trade customers receive account-specific pricing based on volume, contract terms, and relationship history; (2) **Approval workflows** — large B2B purchases require multi-level approval (procurement manager, finance director, C-suite) before order placement; (3) **Credit terms** — B2B transactions typically involve net-30/60/90 payment terms rather than immediate payment; (4) **Catalogue complexity** — B2B catalogues may contain millions of SKUs with customer-specific pricing for each; (5) **Integration requirements** — B2B buyers expect supplier platforms to integrate with their ERP (SAP, Oracle) and procurement systems (Ariba, Coupa). B2B e-commerce platforms must accommodate all five dimensions to replace traditional sales rep-driven ordering.
- **Example**: Amazon Business — launched 2015, reaching $35B+ in annual GMV by 2023 — has become the largest B2B e-commerce platform in the US by enabling business customers to purchase from Amazon's marketplace with business-specific features: multi-user accounts, approval workflows, tax-exempt purchasing, and business pricing (typically 5–15% below consumer pricing on identical items). Amazon Business serves 6M+ registered business customers globally, including 96 of the Fortune 100 companies (Amazon Annual Report, 2024).
- **Business Implication**: A retail operations manager or procurement director who has not evaluated Amazon Business as a supplementary sourcing channel for office supplies, packaging, and MRO items is leaving measurable cost savings on the table. Amazon Business's 5–15% price advantage over traditional distributors on commodity items, combined with next-day delivery and no minimum order requirements, makes it the default choice for unplanned or low-volume B2B purchases — even for organisations with primary distributor relationships.

**Alibaba.com**
- **Definition**: The world's largest B2B e-commerce platform, connecting manufacturers (primarily in China, India, and Southeast Asia) with business buyers globally, facilitating wholesale and manufacturing sourcing transactions with a GMV of approximately $50B+ annually.
- **Mechanics**: Alibaba.com operates as a marketplace where manufacturers and trading companies list products with minimum order quantities (MOQs), wholesale pricing tiers, and customisation options. Buyers can request quotes (RFQ), negotiate pricing, and place orders directly through the platform. Alibaba.com's **Trade Assurance** programme provides payment protection (refund if products don't meet specifications) and quality inspection services, reducing the risk of cross-border B2B transactions. Alibaba.com charges suppliers a membership fee ($2,000–$10,000/year) plus transaction fees (approximately 3–5% of GMV) rather than charging buyers.
- **Example**: A US specialty retailer sourcing private label kitchenware from China uses Alibaba.com to identify manufacturers, request samples, negotiate pricing, and place orders. The retailer searches for "stainless steel cookware manufacturer" on Alibaba.com, receives 500+ supplier listings with MOQs ranging from 100 to 10,000 units and prices ranging from $8–$25 per unit (vs. $40–$80 retail). The retailer requests samples from 5 suppliers ($50–$200 per sample set), selects the best quality/price combination, and places an initial order of 500 units at $12/unit ($6,000 total) with Trade Assurance protection. Alibaba.com's platform reduces the retailer's sourcing cost by approximately 40–60% vs. using a traditional import agent (Alibaba.com Annual Report, 2024; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A merchant developing a private label programme should use Alibaba.com for initial supplier discovery and sample evaluation, but should not rely on it as the sole sourcing channel for production orders above $50,000. Alibaba.com's Trade Assurance provides payment protection but does not guarantee quality consistency across production runs — merchants who skip factory audits and quality inspection on Alibaba.com orders risk receiving production batches that differ materially from approved samples, with limited recourse beyond Trade Assurance refunds.

**Amazon Business**
- **Definition**: Amazon's B2B marketplace platform offering business-specific features (multi-user accounts, approval workflows, tax-exempt purchasing, business pricing) to registered business customers, competing directly with traditional B2B distributors and GPOs (Group Purchasing Organisations).
- **Mechanics**: Amazon Business generates revenue through three streams: (1) **Marketplace commissions** — 8–15% of GMV from third-party sellers; (2) **Amazon Business Prime** — subscription fee ($179–$10,099/year depending on account size) for free shipping, enhanced analytics, and spend management tools; (3) **First-party sales** — Amazon's own wholesale inventory sold directly to business customers. Amazon Business's competitive advantage is its 250M+ product catalogue (vs. 50,000–500,000 SKUs for traditional distributors), next-day delivery capability, and integration with procurement systems (SAP Ariba, Coupa, Jaggaer).
- **Example**: A hospital system purchasing medical supplies through Amazon Business vs. a traditional GPO (Group Purchasing Organisation like Premier or Vizient) faces a trade-off: Amazon Business offers lower prices on commodity items (office supplies, cleaning products, basic medical supplies) but lacks the clinical expertise, regulatory compliance support, and contract pricing on specialty medical devices that GPOs provide. A hospital system that shifts 20% of its $50M annual supply spend to Amazon Business saves approximately $1.5–3M annually on commodity items — but must maintain its GPO relationship for specialty and regulated products (Amazon Annual Report, 2024; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A procurement manager evaluating Amazon Business should segment their spend into "commodity" (office supplies, packaging, cleaning products — where Amazon Business wins on price and convenience) and "strategic" (specialty products requiring compliance documentation, technical support, or regulatory tracking — where vertical distributors win on expertise). A hybrid sourcing strategy that routes commodity spend through Amazon Business and strategic spend through vertical distributors typically generates 8–12% total procurement cost reduction without sacrificing service quality on critical categories.

**Vertical-Specific B2B Platforms**
- **Definition**: Industry-specific B2B e-commerce platforms serving the unique procurement needs of a particular sector — foodservice, construction, healthcare, industrial — with specialised catalogues, compliance features, and integration with sector-specific ERP systems.
- **Mechanics**: Vertical-specific platforms compete with horizontal platforms (Amazon Business, Alibaba.com) by offering deeper category expertise, regulatory compliance features, and integration with industry-specific systems. Examples include: **Sysco Shop** (foodservice), **Grainger.com** (industrial MRO), **McKesson Connect** (pharmaceutical distribution), **BuildingConnected** (construction procurement). These platforms generate higher gross margins (15–25%) than horizontal platforms (8–12%) by providing value-added services (compliance documentation, technical support, regulatory tracking) that justify premium pricing.
- **Example**: Sysco's digital ordering platform — Sysco Shop — processes approximately 65% of Sysco's $76B in annual revenue through digital channels (FY2023), up from 40% in 2019. Sysco Shop integrates with restaurant POS systems (Toast, Square) to enable automatic reordering based on sales data, reducing restaurant operators' ordering time by approximately 2–3 hours per week. Sysco's digital penetration generates approximately $500M in annual cost savings through reduced order-taking labour and improved order accuracy — savings that Sysco shares with customers through lower prices and with shareholders through improved margins (Sysco Annual Report, 2023).
- **Business Implication**: A restaurant operator or retailer who orders from a distributor by phone or through a sales rep rather than through a digital platform is paying a hidden premium — the distributor's order-taking cost ($5–$15 per order for phone/rep-managed orders vs. $0.50–$1.50 for digital orders) is embedded in the product price. Operators who shift to digital ordering platforms capture this cost reduction as lower prices or improved service levels, and gain access to automatic reordering, spend analytics, and menu planning tools that phone-based ordering cannot provide.

> **Key Takeaway**: B2B e-commerce is compressing distributor margins by making price comparison trivial and reducing the value of traditional sales rep relationships. Distributors that compete on price alone will be disintermediated by Amazon Business and Alibaba.com; distributors that compete on value-added services (technical expertise, regulatory compliance, system integration, just-in-time delivery) maintain defensible margins. The shift to B2B e-commerce is not optional — it is a structural transformation that will eliminate distributors who fail to digitise their customer experience.

## 11. B2B Pricing and Credit

### 11.1 Trade Pricing Structures

**Tiered Pricing for Trade Customers**
- **Definition**: A B2B pricing model in which the unit price decreases as order volume increases, structured in defined tiers (e.g., 1–99 units at $10.00, 100–499 units at $8.50, 500+ units at $7.00), incentivising larger orders and rewarding high-volume customers with lower unit costs.
- **Mechanics**: Tiered pricing serves three commercial objectives: (1) **Volume incentive** — encourages customers to consolidate purchases with a single supplier to reach higher tiers; (2) **Cost recovery** — lower unit prices at higher volumes reflect the supplier's lower per-unit cost (fixed costs spread over more units, lower order processing cost per unit); (3) **Customer segmentation** — different tiers effectively segment customers by size and purchasing power without requiring individual price negotiation. Tiered pricing is typically published in a **price list** or **trade catalogue** and is available to all qualifying customers — unlike **contract pricing**, which is negotiated individually.
- **Example**: A beverage distributor supplying independent restaurants uses a three-tier pricing structure: (1) **Standard trade** (1–5 cases/order): $28.00/case; (2) **Volume trade** (6–20 cases/order): $25.50/case; (3) **Key account** (21+ cases/order): $23.00/case. A restaurant ordering 25 cases per week pays $23.00/case vs. $28.00/case for a restaurant ordering 3 cases per week — a 17.9% price difference that reflects the distributor's lower per-unit delivery cost ($15/delivery for 25 cases vs. $15/delivery for 3 cases = $0.60/case vs. $5.00/case delivery cost). The distributor's gross margin is approximately equal across tiers because the lower price is offset by lower delivery cost (approximate benchmark (2024); see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A restaurant operator or retailer who consolidates purchases with a single distributor to reach the highest pricing tier — rather than splitting orders across multiple suppliers to compare prices — typically achieves a better net cost outcome. The 17.9% price difference between the standard and key account tier in the example above is worth $260/week on a 25-case order — $13,500 annually — which exceeds the value of any price comparison benefit from splitting orders across two distributors.

**Contract Pricing**
- **Definition**: A negotiated, account-specific price agreement between a supplier and a key account customer, typically covering a defined product list at fixed prices for a specified period (6–24 months), in exchange for volume commitments or exclusivity.
- **Mechanics**: Contract pricing is the primary commercial tool for managing key account relationships in B2B retail. The negotiation process involves: (1) **Volume commitment** — the customer commits to purchasing a minimum volume (e.g., $500,000 annually) in exchange for the contracted price; (2) **Price protection** — the supplier agrees not to increase prices during the contract period (or to provide advance notice of price increases); (3) **Exclusivity** — in some contracts, the customer agrees to source a defined percentage of their requirements from the supplier; (4) **Rebates** — volume-based rebates (e.g., 2% rebate on purchases exceeding $1M annually) are paid at year-end, effectively reducing the net price for high-volume customers.
- **Example**: Sysco's contract pricing with a national restaurant chain (e.g., Darden Restaurants, which operates Olive Garden and LongHorn Steakhouse) covers approximately 2,000 SKUs at fixed prices for 12 months. Darden's annual food and beverage spend of approximately $2B+ gives it significant negotiating leverage — Sysco offers Darden prices approximately 8–12% below its standard trade price list in exchange for Darden's volume commitment and multi-year relationship. Sysco's gross margin on Darden's account is approximately 10–12% vs. 18–22% on smaller independent restaurant accounts — lower margin but higher volume and lower cost-to-serve (Sysco Annual Report, 2023; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A buyer negotiating contract pricing with a distributor should always offer a volume commitment in exchange for a price reduction — even if the commitment is achievable at current purchasing levels. A $500,000 annual volume commitment that the buyer would have spent anyway generates an 8–12% price reduction worth $40,000–$60,000 annually at no incremental cost. Buyers who purchase on standard trade price lists without negotiating contract pricing are systematically overpaying relative to their actual purchasing power.

### 11.2 Credit Management

**Trade Credit**
- **Definition**: The extension of credit by a supplier to a business customer, allowing the customer to receive goods and pay within a defined period (net-30, net-60, net-90 days) rather than at the point of purchase, representing the primary financing mechanism in B2B commerce.
- **Mechanics**: Trade credit management involves: (1) **Credit assessment** — evaluating the customer's creditworthiness (payment history, financial statements, credit bureau reports) before extending credit; (2) **Credit limit setting** — establishing the maximum outstanding balance the customer may carry; (3) **Invoice terms** — specifying the payment due date and any early payment discounts (e.g., "2/10 net 30" = 2% discount if paid within 10 days, full amount due in 30 days); (4) **Collections** — managing overdue accounts through reminder notices, account holds, and collections agencies. Trade credit is a significant working capital investment for suppliers: a distributor with $100M in annual B2B sales and net-30 terms has approximately $8.2M in accounts receivable outstanding at any time ($100M / 365 × 30 = $8.2M).

```
Trade Credit Economics:
  Annual B2B Sales:          $100,000,000
  Average Payment Terms:     Net-45 days
  Average Accounts Receivable: $100M / 365 × 45 = $12,329,000
  Cost of Capital (8%):      $12,329,000 × 8% = $986,000/year
  Bad Debt Rate (0.5%):      $100M × 0.5% = $500,000/year
  Total Credit Cost:         $1,486,000/year (1.49% of sales)
  
  Early Payment Discount (2/10 net 30):
    If 30% of customers take the discount:
    Discount Cost: $100M × 30% × 2% = $600,000/year
    Working Capital Benefit: Reduces AR by $100M × 30% / 365 × 20 days = $1,644,000
    Net Benefit of Discount Programme: Positive if cost of capital > 2% / 20 days = 36.5% annualised
```

- **Example**: Metro AG's trade credit programme — offering net-30 payment terms to registered business customers with credit limits of €5,000–€500,000 depending on customer size and payment history — generates approximately €2.5B in accounts receivable outstanding at any time. Metro's bad debt rate of approximately 0.3–0.5% of credit sales reflects its rigorous credit assessment process (requiring 2 years of financial statements and trade references for credit limits above €50,000). Metro's credit management team of approximately 500 specialists across 35 countries manages the credit risk of 21 million registered customers (Metro AG Annual Report, 2024; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A B2B supplier who extends trade credit without a formal credit assessment process is taking unquantified financial risk. A bad debt rate of 1% on $50M in annual credit sales generates $500,000 in annual write-offs — equivalent to the gross profit on $2.5M in sales at a 20% gross margin. Suppliers should implement a tiered credit assessment process: automatic approval for credit limits below $5,000 (based on trade references), financial statement review for $5,000–$50,000 limits, and full credit analysis for limits above $50,000.

**Invoice Terms and Early Payment Discounts**
- **Definition**: The payment terms specified on a B2B invoice, including the due date (net-30, net-60, net-90), any early payment discount (2/10 net 30), and any late payment penalty, governing the timing of cash flow between supplier and customer.
- **Mechanics**: Invoice terms are a negotiated element of the B2B commercial relationship. Large customers (Walmart, Amazon, major restaurant chains) routinely negotiate extended payment terms (net-60 to net-120) as a form of supplier financing — effectively using supplier credit to fund their own working capital. Suppliers who accept extended terms must fund the working capital gap through their own credit facilities, increasing their cost of capital. **Dynamic discounting** — a fintech solution that allows suppliers to offer variable early payment discounts based on the customer's cash position — is increasingly used to optimise the trade-off between early payment discounts and working capital cost.
- **Example**: Walmart's standard payment terms for suppliers are net-60 days — significantly longer than the industry standard of net-30. On Walmart's $500B+ in annual merchandise purchases, net-60 terms generate approximately $82B in supplier-financed working capital ($500B / 365 × 60 = $82B). Walmart's suppliers effectively provide Walmart with $82B in interest-free financing — a significant competitive advantage that reduces Walmart's working capital requirements. Suppliers who cannot afford to extend net-60 terms to Walmart can use Walmart's **Supplier Financing Programme** (in partnership with HSBC and others) to receive early payment at a discount of approximately 1–2% annually (Walmart Annual Report, 2024; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A supplier negotiating payment terms with a large retailer (Walmart, Amazon, Costco) should model the working capital cost of extended terms before agreeing to them. Accepting net-90 terms from a $10M annual customer at an 8% cost of capital costs the supplier approximately $197,000 annually in financing cost — a hidden price reduction that must be recovered through higher product pricing or absorbed as a margin reduction. Suppliers who use dynamic discounting platforms (C2FO, Taulia) can offer early payment discounts selectively to customers whose extended terms create the highest working capital burden.

> **Key Takeaway**: Trade credit is a hidden cost in B2B commerce that is frequently underestimated by suppliers. A supplier extending net-60 terms to a $10M annual customer is providing approximately $1.6M in interest-free financing — at an 8% cost of capital, this represents $130,000 in annual financing cost that must be recovered through pricing. B2B suppliers who do not model the cost of trade credit into their pricing systematically undercharge large customers who demand extended terms.

## 12. B2B Customer Relationship Management

### 12.1 Account Management and Key Account Strategy

**B2B Account Management**
- **Definition**: The structured commercial relationship management process by which a supplier assigns dedicated account managers to business customers, responsible for maintaining the relationship, growing the account, resolving issues, and ensuring customer retention.
- **Mechanics**: B2B account management operates on a tiered model: (1) **Key accounts** (top 20% of customers by revenue, typically generating 80% of supplier revenue) — assigned dedicated account managers with authority to negotiate pricing, terms, and service levels; (2) **Mid-tier accounts** — managed by territory sales representatives who cover multiple accounts; (3) **Small accounts** — managed through inside sales (phone/email) or self-service digital channels. Account manager productivity is measured by revenue per account manager, account retention rate, and share of wallet (percentage of the customer's total category spend captured by the supplier).
- **Example**: Sysco's account management structure — approximately 10,000 sales associates managing 700,000+ customer accounts — is the largest B2B sales force in foodservice distribution. Sysco's key account managers (covering national restaurant chains) manage accounts generating $10M–$500M+ in annual revenue and are compensated on a combination of revenue growth, gross profit growth, and customer retention. Sysco's customer retention rate of approximately 90% annually — driven by its account management investment — generates approximately $68B in recurring annual revenue from existing customers (Sysco Annual Report, 2023; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A B2B supplier who assigns the same account manager to a $5M customer and a $50,000 customer is misallocating its most expensive commercial resource. Account management should be tiered: dedicated account managers for customers generating >$1M annually, territory sales representatives for $100,000–$1M customers, and digital self-service for customers below $100,000. This tiering reduces cost-to-serve for small accounts while concentrating relationship investment where the revenue at risk justifies it.

**Key Account Strategy**
- **Definition**: A structured approach to managing the supplier's most commercially significant customers, involving dedicated resources (account manager, customer service team, technical support), customised service levels, and joint business planning to grow the relationship and defend against competitive threats.
- **Mechanics**: Key account strategy involves four elements: (1) **Account segmentation** — identifying which accounts qualify as "key" based on current revenue, growth potential, and strategic importance; (2) **Joint business planning (JBP)** — annual planning sessions where supplier and customer agree on shared revenue, volume, and service targets for the coming year; (3) **Customised service** — dedicated delivery schedules, custom product specifications, priority customer service; (4) **Executive sponsorship** — senior supplier executives maintain relationships with senior customer executives, ensuring the relationship is not dependent on a single account manager. Key account management is expensive — a dedicated key account team for a $50M customer costs approximately $500,000–$1M annually — but the cost is justified by the revenue at risk if the account is lost.
- **Example**: Unilever's key account management for Walmart — one of Unilever's top 5 global customers, generating approximately $5B+ in annual sales — involves a dedicated team of approximately 200 people based in Bentonville, Arkansas (Walmart's headquarters). Unilever's Walmart team includes account managers, category managers, supply chain specialists, and data analysts who work exclusively on the Walmart relationship. Unilever and Walmart conduct annual Joint Business Planning sessions that set shared targets for category growth, promotional investment, and supply chain efficiency. Unilever's investment in the Walmart relationship — approximately $20–30M annually in dedicated team costs — is justified by the $5B+ in annual revenue at stake (Unilever Annual Report, 2023; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A supplier who conducts Joint Business Planning with its top 10 accounts — setting shared revenue, volume, and service targets — generates measurably higher account retention and share of wallet growth than suppliers who manage accounts reactively. JBP creates mutual accountability: the customer commits to volume targets; the supplier commits to service levels and pricing. Suppliers who skip JBP with key accounts leave the relationship vulnerable to competitive displacement when a rival offers a lower price without the context of a shared growth plan.

**Share of Wallet**
- **Definition**: The percentage of a customer's total spend in a category that is captured by a single supplier, the primary metric for measuring account penetration and identifying growth opportunities within existing customer relationships.
- **Mechanics**: Share of wallet analysis requires understanding the customer's total category spend (not just the spend with the supplier) — information that is often obtained through customer surveys, industry data, or direct customer disclosure during joint business planning. A supplier with 30% share of wallet in a $10M customer has $7M in potential incremental revenue if it can increase share to 100% — a more capital-efficient growth opportunity than acquiring a new $7M customer. Share of wallet growth strategies include: (1) **Category expansion** — adding new product categories to the existing relationship; (2) **Exclusivity agreements** — negotiating preferred supplier status in exchange for pricing or service commitments; (3) **System integration** — integrating the supplier's ordering system with the customer's ERP to create switching costs.
- **Example**: Grainger's share of wallet strategy for its industrial MRO (Maintenance, Repair, and Operations) customers focuses on "KeepStock" — a vendor-managed inventory programme where Grainger manages the customer's on-site MRO inventory, automatically replenishing items as they are consumed. KeepStock customers spend approximately 2.5x more with Grainger than non-KeepStock customers and have a retention rate of approximately 95% vs. 75% for non-KeepStock customers. Grainger's KeepStock programme covers approximately 30,000 customer locations and generates approximately $3B in annual revenue — demonstrating that share of wallet growth through service integration is more durable than price-based competition (Grainger Annual Report, 2023; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A B2B supplier whose share of wallet with its top 20 accounts is below 40% has a significant organic growth opportunity that requires no new customer acquisition. Increasing share of wallet from 30% to 50% in a $5M account generates $1M in incremental revenue — at a cost-to-serve of approximately 5–10% of incremental revenue (vs. 20–30% for new customer acquisition). Share of wallet growth through VMI, category expansion, and system integration is the highest-ROI growth strategy available to established B2B suppliers.

> **Key Takeaway**: B2B account management is a revenue protection investment, not a cost centre. A supplier who loses a $10M key account to a competitor loses not just $10M in revenue but also the gross profit, the overhead absorption, and the reference value of the relationship. The cost of retaining a key account (dedicated account manager: $150,000–$300,000/year) is a fraction of the cost of replacing the revenue — making key account investment one of the highest-ROI commercial activities in B2B retail.

## 13. Wholesale Distribution

### 13.1 Distribution Models and Channel Economics

**Distributor vs. Wholesaler**
- **Definition**: A **distributor** is a company that purchases products from manufacturers, holds inventory, and sells to retailers or end-users with value-added services (sales force, credit, delivery, technical support); a **wholesaler** is a company that purchases products in bulk and resells them at a markup without necessarily providing the full range of services a distributor offers.
- **Mechanics**: The distinction between distributor and wholesaler is primarily one of service level and value-add: (1) **Distributors** typically have exclusive or semi-exclusive territorial agreements with manufacturers, provide dedicated sales representatives, extend trade credit, manage inventory on behalf of customers, and offer technical support; (2) **Wholesalers** typically operate on a non-exclusive basis, sell to any buyer, offer limited services, and compete primarily on price. In practice, the terms are often used interchangeably, but the distinction matters for margin analysis: distributors earn gross margins of 15–25% (reflecting their service investment) while wholesalers earn 8–15% (reflecting their lower service cost).
- **Example**: Sysco (foodservice distributor) vs. Restaurant Depot (foodservice wholesaler/cash & carry) illustrates the distinction. Sysco provides dedicated sales representatives, next-day delivery, credit terms (net-30), custom product specifications, and menu consulting — services that justify its gross margin of approximately 18–22%. Restaurant Depot operates a cash & carry model (no delivery, no credit, no sales reps) with gross margins of approximately 10–14% — lower margins that enable prices approximately 10–15% below Sysco. A restaurant operator choosing between Sysco and Restaurant Depot is trading service and convenience (Sysco) for lower prices (Restaurant Depot) — a decision that depends on the operator's labour cost, storage capacity, and ordering frequency (Sysco Annual Report, 2023; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A manufacturer evaluating its distribution strategy should distinguish between distributors (who provide sales force, credit, and service infrastructure that justifies a 15–25% margin) and wholesalers (who provide price-competitive bulk access at 8–15% margin but limited service). Using a distributor for a category that requires no technical support, no credit management, and no sales force is paying a 7–10 percentage point margin premium for services that generate no value — a cost that should be eliminated by shifting to direct-to-retailer or wholesale distribution.

**Direct-to-Retailer vs. Through Distribution**
- **Definition**: Two competing go-to-market models for manufacturers: **direct-to-retailer (DTR)** involves the manufacturer selling and delivering directly to retail accounts without intermediaries; **through distribution** involves the manufacturer selling to distributors who then sell to retailers, adding a distribution margin (typically 15–25%) but reducing the manufacturer's cost-to-serve.
- **Mechanics**: The DTR vs. through-distribution decision is driven by: (1) **Account size** — large retailers (Walmart, Kroger, Target) justify direct relationships because the volume supports the manufacturer's direct sales and logistics cost; (2) **Geographic coverage** — distributors provide coverage in markets where the manufacturer cannot cost-effectively maintain a direct sales force; (3) **Category complexity** — categories requiring technical support, cold chain management, or frequent replenishment (fresh food, beverages) often require distributor infrastructure; (4) **Margin economics** — DTR captures the distributor's 15–25% margin but requires the manufacturer to invest in sales force, logistics, and credit management.

```
DTR vs. Through Distribution Economics:
  Manufacturer's selling price to distributor:  $10.00/unit
  Distributor's selling price to retailer:       $12.50/unit (25% markup)
  Retailer's selling price to consumer:          $18.75/unit (50% markup)
  
  DTR model:
  Manufacturer's selling price to retailer:      $12.50/unit (captures distributor margin)
  Manufacturer's direct sales/logistics cost:    $1.50/unit
  Net manufacturer margin improvement:           $1.00/unit (10% improvement)
  
  Break-even: DTR is profitable when direct sales/logistics cost < distributor margin
  At $1.50/unit direct cost vs. $2.50/unit distributor margin: DTR saves $1.00/unit
```

- **Example**: Coca-Cola's distribution model combines DTR and through-distribution based on account size. Coca-Cola's **Direct Store Delivery (DSD)** model — where Coca-Cola's own delivery trucks deliver directly to large grocery, convenience, and foodservice accounts — covers approximately 80% of Coca-Cola's US volume. Coca-Cola's DSD model enables it to manage shelf placement, ensure product freshness (FIFO rotation), and maintain promotional compliance at the store level — capabilities that distributor-managed delivery cannot replicate. For smaller accounts (independent restaurants, small convenience stores), Coca-Cola uses independent distributors who aggregate orders and deliver on a less frequent schedule. Coca-Cola's DSD investment — approximately $2–3B annually in US distribution infrastructure — is justified by the shelf placement and freshness control it enables (Coca-Cola Annual Report, 2023; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).
- **Business Implication**: A manufacturer whose top 20 retail accounts represent 60%+ of volume should evaluate DTR for those accounts — the distributor's 15–25% margin on high-volume, low-complexity accounts is a cost that DTR eliminates. The break-even point for DTR vs. through-distribution is typically when a single account generates $2M+ in annual purchases — at that volume, the manufacturer's direct sales and logistics cost ($150,000–$300,000 annually) is less than the distributor's margin ($300,000–$500,000 annually). Accounts below $500,000 in annual purchases almost always justify through-distribution.

**Distributor Value-Added Services**
- **Definition**: Services provided by distributors beyond product delivery — including inventory management, demand forecasting, category management, regulatory compliance support, and technology integration — that justify the distributor's margin premium over direct manufacturer pricing.
- **Mechanics**: Value-added services are the primary defence against disintermediation by B2B e-commerce platforms. Distributors who compete only on price will be undercut by Amazon Business and direct manufacturer e-commerce; distributors who provide services that reduce the customer's total cost of ownership (not just purchase price) maintain defensible margins. The most valuable distributor services are those that are deeply integrated into the customer's operations — vendor-managed inventory (VMI), EDI ordering, and ERP integration create switching costs that price competition cannot easily overcome.
- **Example**: McKesson Corporation — the largest pharmaceutical distributor in the US with $276B in annual revenue (FY2023) — provides independent pharmacies with services that go far beyond drug delivery: (1) **Pharmacy management software** (McKesson EnterpriseRx) — a full pharmacy management system provided at below-market cost to McKesson customers; (2) **Reimbursement support** — helping pharmacies navigate complex PBM reimbursement contracts; (3) **Regulatory compliance** — DEA compliance support for controlled substance management; (4) **Group purchasing** — aggregating independent pharmacy purchasing power to negotiate manufacturer pricing comparable to large chains. McKesson's gross margin of approximately 3–4% on pharmaceutical distribution is thin, but its value-added services generate approximately $2–3B in additional annual revenue at margins of 20–30% (McKesson Annual Report, 2023).
- **Business Implication**: A distributor who cannot articulate the dollar value of its value-added services to each customer — in terms of labour hours saved, inventory investment reduced, compliance risk mitigated — will lose those customers to Amazon Business or direct manufacturer e-commerce when price comparison becomes trivial. Distributors should quantify their service value annually in a "Total Cost of Partnership" analysis for each key account, demonstrating that the distributor's margin premium is less than the cost of replicating those services independently.

> **Key Takeaway**: The distributor's value proposition is not product access — it is the bundle of services that reduce the customer's total cost of ownership. Distributors who can demonstrate that their services (VMI, compliance support, system integration) reduce the customer's labour cost, inventory investment, and regulatory risk by more than the distributor's margin premium will retain their position in the supply chain. Distributors who cannot make this case will be disintermediated by B2B e-commerce platforms.

## 15. Practical Application — How Leading Operators Execute These Models

### 15.1 Franchise Operations: McDonald's Franchise System Management

McDonald's franchise system management is the global benchmark for balancing franchisee autonomy with brand consistency at scale. McDonald's 95% franchised model generates approximately $15B in annual franchisor revenue (royalties + rent + fees) on $112B+ in system-wide sales — a capital-light model that generates ROIC of approximately 30–35%.

McDonald's franchise management infrastructure includes: (1) **Field operations** — approximately 1,600 field consultants globally, each managing 20–25 franchisee locations, conducting quarterly Business Reviews and providing operational coaching; (2) **Technology monitoring** — real-time POS data, kitchen display system data, and drive-through timer data monitored centrally, with automated alerts for deviations from standards; (3) **Hamburger University** — McDonald's global training centre (Oak Brook, IL + 7 international campuses) that has trained 275,000+ franchise operators and managers since 1961; (4) **Franchisee advisory councils** — regional and national franchisee advisory boards that provide input on menu, marketing, and operational decisions, creating a governance structure that balances franchisor control with franchisee voice.

McDonald's franchisee selection process — accepting approximately 1 in 20 applicants — ensures that new franchisees have the financial capacity ($500,000+ in liquid assets), operational experience, and cultural alignment to succeed. McDonald's franchisee retention rate of approximately 95% annually (franchisees who renew vs. exit) reflects the strength of its franchise economics and support infrastructure (McDonald's Annual Report, 2023).

### 15.2 Franchise Expansion: 7-Eleven's Conversion Model

7-Eleven's franchise growth strategy — converting company-owned stores to franchise operations — is the most capital-efficient franchise expansion model in convenience retail. 7-Eleven converted approximately 6,000 US stores from company-owned to franchise between 2000 and 2020, generating approximately $1.5–2.0B in annual royalty income while eliminating the operating risk of company-owned stores.

7-Eleven's franchise model is unique in its profit-sharing structure: rather than a fixed royalty percentage, 7-Eleven franchisees pay 34–52% of gross profit to 7-Eleven (the percentage varies based on store volume and location type). This structure aligns franchisor and franchisee incentives — 7-Eleven earns more when franchisees earn more — but creates complexity in financial planning for franchisees. 7-Eleven's AUV of approximately $1.5M generates approximately $510,000–$780,000 in annual gross profit sharing to 7-Eleven per location.

7-Eleven's site selection advantage — its real estate team controls the lease for approximately 80% of US locations — gives it significant leverage over franchisee compliance and location quality. Franchisees who fail to meet brand standards risk losing their lease (and their investment) in addition to their franchise agreement — a dual compliance mechanism that is unique to 7-Eleven's model (7-Eleven FDD, 2023; approximate benchmark; see Corpus Sourcing Note in 00-table-of-contents.md).

### 15.3 B2B Commerce: Sysco's Digital Transformation

Sysco's digital transformation — shifting from a sales rep-driven ordering model to a digital-first platform — is the most significant B2B commerce transformation in foodservice distribution. Sysco's digital ordering platform (Sysco Shop) processed approximately 65% of Sysco's $76B in annual revenue in FY2023, up from 40% in 2019 — a shift that generated approximately $500M in annual cost savings through reduced order-taking labour and improved order accuracy.

Sysco's digital strategy has three components: (1) **Sysco Shop** — a customer-facing ordering platform that integrates with restaurant POS systems (Toast, Square, Lightspeed) to enable automatic reordering based on sales data; (2) **Sysco Studio** — a menu planning and costing tool that helps restaurant operators design menus, calculate food costs, and identify Sysco products that meet their specifications; (3) **Sysco Analytics** — a business intelligence platform that provides restaurant operators with benchmarking data (food cost vs. industry average, menu item profitability) that helps them improve their operations.

Sysco's digital investment — approximately $1B+ over 5 years — has generated measurable commercial results: digital customers spend approximately 20% more with Sysco than non-digital customers, have a retention rate of approximately 93% vs. 85% for non-digital customers, and require approximately 40% less sales rep time to manage. Sysco's operating margin of approximately 3.5–4.0% — thin by retail standards but strong for distribution — reflects the efficiency gains from digital transformation (Sysco Annual Report, 2023).

### 15.4 Wholesale B2B: Metro AG's Trade Customer Ecosystem

Metro AG's trade customer ecosystem — serving 21 million registered business customers across 680+ cash & carry stores in 35 countries — is the most comprehensive B2B wholesale operation in the world. Metro's strategy of focusing exclusively on trade customers (restaurants, hotels, caterers, independent retailers) differentiates it from consumer retailers who serve both B2B and B2C customers.

Metro's competitive advantage is its **METRO Chef** private label programme — approximately 2,000 SKUs of foodservice-specific products (catering pack sizes, professional-grade quality) that are not available in consumer retail. METRO Chef products generate gross margins of approximately 25–30% vs. 12–15% for branded products, and create a differentiation that Amazon Business and consumer cash & carry operators cannot replicate. Metro's METRO Chef penetration of approximately 20% of food sales generates approximately €1.2B in annual private label revenue at margins 10–15 percentage points above branded equivalents.

Metro's digital transformation — Metro Markets (B2B e-commerce platform) and the METRO App (mobile ordering for trade customers) — processed approximately 15% of Metro's revenue through digital channels in FY2023, up from 5% in 2019. Metro's digital customers have an average transaction value approximately 30% higher than in-store customers, reflecting the convenience of digital ordering for large, planned purchases (Metro AG Annual Report, 2024).

## 17. Case Studies

### Case Study 17.1: McDonald's — The Gold Standard of Franchise System Management

**Retailer/Franchisor**: McDonald's Corporation, USA, QSR format, 40,275 locations globally (95% franchised), $25.5B franchisor revenue / $112B+ system-wide sales (FY2023).

**Challenge**: McDonald's faced two simultaneous pressures in 2019–2023: (1) declining drive-through speed of service (average 5.5 minutes in 2020 vs. 3.5 minutes in 2015) due to menu complexity expansion; (2) franchisee dissatisfaction with the cost of the "Experience of the Future" (EOTF) remodel programme ($300,000–$700,000 per location), which was required for franchise renewal.

**Approach**: McDonald's "Accelerating the Arches" strategy (2020–present) addressed both challenges: (1) **Menu simplification** — reducing the US menu by approximately 30% (eliminating all-day breakfast, reducing limited-time offers) to improve kitchen throughput; (2) **Technology investment** — deploying AI-powered digital menu boards (Dynamic Yield, acquired 2019) across 8,000+ US locations, increasing average check by $0.50–0.75 per transaction; (3) **Franchisee support** — providing $1B+ in franchisee financial assistance during COVID-19 (rent deferrals, royalty reductions), strengthening the franchisor-franchisee relationship; (4) **Digital loyalty** — launching MyMcDonald's Rewards (150M+ global members), driving 2–3x visit frequency among loyalty members.

**Outcome**: McDonald's US SSSG of 8.7% in 2023 — driven by menu price increases and digital ordering growth — generated approximately $9.7B in incremental system-wide sales vs. 2022. McDonald's digital sales reached $22B globally (40% of system sales) in FY2023. Franchisee satisfaction improved significantly following COVID-19 financial support — McDonald's National Franchisee Leadership Alliance (NFLA) satisfaction survey showed a 15-point improvement in franchisor-franchisee relationship scores between 2020 and 2022. McDonald's stock price increased from $185 (January 2020) to $295 (December 2023) — a 59% increase reflecting the strength of its franchise system economics.

**Lesson**: McDonald's demonstrates that franchise system health requires continuous investment in both franchisee economics (ensuring franchisees earn acceptable returns) and brand standards (ensuring customers receive consistent experiences). Franchisors who prioritise royalty extraction over franchisee profitability generate short-term income at the cost of long-term system health.

### Case Study 17.2: Subway — Franchise System Contraction and Recovery

**Retailer/Franchisor**: Subway (Doctor's Associates Inc.), USA, QSR/fast casual format, 37,000+ locations globally (100% franchised), estimated $10B+ system-wide sales (FY2023).

**Challenge**: Subway's rapid expansion to 44,000+ global locations (peak 2015) created a franchise system with structural economic problems: (1) AUV of approximately $420,000 — insufficient to generate acceptable franchisee returns after Subway's 12.5% total fee burden; (2) Franchisee dissatisfaction scores among the lowest in the IFA's annual survey; (3) Brand perception declining due to inconsistent food quality and the Jared Fogle scandal (2015); (4) Competitive pressure from Chipotle, Jersey Mike's, and Firehouse Subs eroding Subway's fast casual positioning.

**Approach**: Subway's recovery strategy (2021–present) under CEO John Chidsey involved: (1) **Network rationalisation** — allowing approximately 7,000 underperforming US locations to close (2015–2022), improving system AUV by eliminating the lowest-performing units; (2) **"Eat Fresh Refresh" brand overhaul** (2021) — new menu items (Footlong Cookies, Rotisserie-Style Chicken), updated store design, and a $80M marketing campaign; (3) **Franchisee economics improvement** — reducing the royalty rate for new franchisees from 8% to 6.5% in select markets, and providing $80M in franchisee financial assistance for store remodels; (4) **Digital investment** — launching a new loyalty programme (Subway MVP Rewards) and improving the digital ordering platform.

**Outcome**: Subway's US SSSG of +7% in 2021 and +9% in 2022 — the strongest consecutive SSSG performance in Subway's history — demonstrated the effectiveness of the brand refresh. Subway's AUV increased from approximately $420,000 (2019) to approximately $450,000 (2023) — a 7% improvement driven by the combination of network rationalisation (removing low-AUV units) and same-store sales growth. Subway's franchisee satisfaction scores improved from the bottom quartile (2019) to the second quartile (2023) in the Franchise Business Review annual survey. Subway's sale to Roark Capital Group (2023, estimated $9.6B) — the largest franchise acquisition in history — validated the recovery strategy.

**Lesson**: Franchise system contraction — closing underperforming units — is a necessary but painful path to system health. Subway's closure of 7,000 US locations improved system economics for remaining franchisees, but required 7 years of execution and significant franchisee financial assistance. Franchisors who allow underperforming units to persist damage the brand and the economics of all franchisees; proactive network management is a strategic imperative.

### Case Study 17.3: Sysco — B2B Distribution Digital Transformation

**Company**: Sysco Corporation, USA, foodservice distribution, 700,000+ customer accounts, $76.3B annual revenue (FY2023).

**Challenge**: Sysco faced structural margin pressure from three directions: (1) Amazon Business entering foodservice distribution with prices 5–10% below Sysco on commodity items; (2) Restaurant operators demanding digital ordering capabilities (mobile app, ERP integration, automatic reordering) that Sysco's legacy sales rep model could not provide; (3) Labour cost inflation increasing the cost of Sysco's 10,000-person sales force by approximately 15–20% between 2021 and 2023.

Digital customers spend approximately 20% more with Sysco than non-digital customers and have a retention rate of 93% vs. 85% for non-digital customers. Sysco's operating margin improved from 2.8% (FY2020) to 3.8% (FY2023) — a 100 basis point improvement driven by digital efficiency gains. Sysco's market share in US foodservice distribution increased from approximately 16% (2019) to approximately 17% (2023), despite Amazon Business's entry into the category.

**Lesson**: B2B distributors who invest in digital platforms that reduce the customer's total cost of ownership (not just purchase price) build switching costs that price-based competitors cannot overcome. Sysco's digital investment generated a 20% increase in customer spend and an 8-point improvement in retention — demonstrating that digital transformation in B2B distribution is a revenue growth strategy, not just a cost reduction initiative.

### Case Study 17.4: Metro AG — Cash & Carry B2B Wholesale at Global Scale

**Company**: Metro AG, Germany, cash & carry wholesale format, 680+ stores in 35 countries, €29.8B annual revenue (FY2023/24).

**Challenge**: Metro faced structural pressure from two directions: (1) Consumer supermarkets (Carrefour, Tesco, Aldi) expanding their B2B offerings, competing with Metro for small business customers; (2) B2B e-commerce platforms (Amazon Business, local competitors) offering digital ordering with delivery, eliminating the "cash & carry" convenience advantage for customers who could not transport bulk purchases.

**Approach**: Metro's "sCore" strategy (2022–2026) focused on three priorities: (1) **Trade customer focus** — exiting consumer retail entirely (selling its consumer hypermarket business in Russia and Eastern Europe) to focus exclusively on professional trade customers (HoReCa: Hotels, Restaurants, Catering); (2) **METRO Chef private label expansion** — growing its foodservice-specific private label from 1,500 to 2,500 SKUs, targeting 25% of food sales by 2026; (3) **Digital and delivery** — launching Metro Markets (B2B e-commerce with next-day delivery) in 10 major European cities, enabling trade customers to order digitally and receive delivery rather than visiting the store.

**Outcome**: Metro's HoReCa customer revenue grew from 55% of total sales (FY2019) to 62% (FY2023) — reflecting the success of its trade customer focus strategy. METRO Chef private label penetration reached 20% of food sales in FY2023, generating approximately €1.2B in annual private label revenue at gross margins 10–15 percentage points above branded equivalents. Metro Markets digital delivery processed approximately 15% of Metro's revenue in FY2023, with digital customers showing 30% higher average transaction values than in-store customers. Metro's operating margin of approximately 3.5% (FY2023) — above its 3.0% target — reflects the margin improvement from private label growth and digital efficiency.

**Lesson**: Cash & carry operators that focus exclusively on trade customers and invest in trade-specific private label and digital capabilities build a defensible competitive position that consumer retailers and B2B e-commerce platforms cannot easily replicate. Metro's exit from consumer retail — a counterintuitive strategic move — enabled it to concentrate resources on the trade customer segment where it has genuine competitive advantage.

| **B2B E-commerce** | The digital sale of products and services between businesses, conducted through online platforms, EDI, or API-based ordering systems; the US B2B e-commerce market is projected to reach $3.0 trillion by 2027. | Business-to-Business Commerce, B2B Digital Commerce |
| **Brand Standards** | The documented specifications for every customer-facing element of a franchise operation — store design, product offerings, service protocols, marketing materials — that franchisees are contractually required to follow to maintain brand consistency. | Operating Standards, Brand Guidelines |
| **Cash & Carry** | A wholesale retail format that sells products in bulk quantities at trade prices exclusively to registered business customers who pay immediately and transport purchases themselves, eliminating delivery and credit costs. | Trade Cash & Carry, Wholesale Club (B2B) |
| **Contract Pricing** | A negotiated, account-specific price agreement between a supplier and a key account customer, covering a defined product list at fixed prices for a specified period in exchange for volume commitments or exclusivity. | Account Pricing, Negotiated Pricing, Key Account Pricing |
| **Days Sales Outstanding (DSO)** | The average number of days it takes a company to collect payment after a sale has been made; calculated as (Accounts Receivable / Annual Revenue) × 365; the primary metric for B2B credit management efficiency. | DSO, Debtor Days, Collection Period |
| **Direct Store Delivery (DSD)** | A distribution model in which a manufacturer delivers products directly to retail store locations using its own delivery fleet, bypassing the retailer's distribution centre; enables shelf management, freshness control, and promotional compliance. | DSD, Direct Delivery |
| **Distributor** | A company that purchases products from manufacturers, holds inventory, and sells to retailers or end-users with value-added services (sales force, credit, delivery, technical support); earns gross margins of 15–25% reflecting its service investment. | Full-Service Distributor, Wholesale Distributor |
| **Dynamic Discounting** | A fintech-enabled programme that allows suppliers to offer variable early payment discounts to buyers based on the buyer's cash position and the supplier's working capital needs; optimises the trade-off between early payment discounts and working capital cost for both parties. | Supply Chain Finance, Early Payment Programme |
| **Encroachment** | The act of a franchisor opening a competing unit (company-owned or franchised) within or adjacent to an existing franchisee's protected or exclusive territory; one of the most common sources of franchisee-franchisor litigation, particularly as digital and delivery channels create new competitive vectors. | Territory Encroachment, Competitive Encroachment |
| **Franchise Agreement** | The legally binding contract between a franchisor and franchisee specifying territory rights, brand standards, fee obligations, training requirements, renewal terms, and termination conditions; the primary governance document of the franchise relationship. | Franchise Contract, FA |
| **Franchise Disclosure Document (FDD)** | A legally mandated disclosure document (23 standardised items) that franchisors must provide to prospective franchisees at least 14 calendar days before any agreement is signed or money paid, as required by the FTC Franchise Rule (16 CFR Part 436). | FDD, Uniform Franchise Offering Circular (UFOC, pre-2008) |
| **Franchisee** | An independent operator who invests capital to operate a franchise unit under the franchisor's brand and systems, bearing operating risk in exchange for the right to use the franchisor's brand, systems, and intellectual property. | Franchise Operator, Franchise Owner |
| **Franchisee Payback Period** | The number of years required for a franchisee to recover their total initial investment from operating profit; calculated as Total Initial Investment / Annual Operating Profit; the primary metric for evaluating franchise investment attractiveness. | **Franchising** | A contractual business model in which a brand owner (franchisor) licences its brand, systems, and intellectual property to independent operators (franchisees) in exchange for fees and royalties, enabling rapid network expansion with minimal franchisor capital investment. | Franchise Model, Business Format Franchising |
| **Franchisor** | The brand owner who develops and licences the franchise system, earning royalties and fees from franchisees in exchange for the right to use the brand, systems, and intellectual property; bears minimal operating risk in a fully franchised system. | Franchise Company, Brand Owner |
| **Group Purchasing Organisation (GPO)** | An entity that aggregates the purchasing power of multiple member organisations (hospitals, restaurants, retailers) to negotiate volume-based pricing and contract terms with suppliers; GPOs like Premier, Vizient (healthcare), and Foodbuy (foodservice) enable small and mid-size buyers to access pricing comparable to large chains. | GPO, Buying Group, Purchasing Consortium |
| **HoReCa** | An industry acronym for Hotels, Restaurants, and Catering — the primary trade customer segment for cash & carry operators (Metro AG, Makro) and foodservice distributors (Sysco, US Foods); HoReCa customers purchase in bulk, require professional-grade products, and prioritise price and reliability over consumer-format packaging. | Foodservice, Food Away From Home (FAFH) |
| **Initial Franchise Fee** | A one-time, non-refundable payment made by the franchisee to the franchisor upon signing the Franchise Agreement, granting the right to operate under the franchisor's brand and systems for the agreement term. | Franchise Fee, Initial Fee |
| **Joint Business Planning (JBP)** | An annual planning process between a supplier and a key account customer in which both parties agree on shared revenue, volume, and service targets for the coming year; the primary tool for managing strategic B2B relationships. | JBP, Annual Business Planning |
| **Key Account** | A customer that represents a disproportionate share of a supplier's revenue (typically top 20% of customers generating 80% of revenue), warranting dedicated account management resources and customised service levels. | Strategic Account, National Account |
| **Master Franchise** | A franchise arrangement in which a master franchisee acquires the rights to an entire country or large region, with the authority to sub-franchise to individual unit operators within that territory, effectively acting as the franchisor within their territory. | Master Franchisee, Sub-Franchisor |
| **METRO Chef** | Metro AG's foodservice-specific private label programme — approximately 2,000 SKUs of catering-pack products — generating gross margins 10–15 percentage points above branded equivalents and differentiating Metro from consumer retailers and B2B e-commerce platforms. | Metro Private Label, Trade Private Label |
| **Minimum Order Quantity (MOQ)** | The smallest quantity of a product that a supplier will sell in a single order; a key parameter in B2B sourcing that determines the minimum inventory investment required to access a supplier's pricing. | MOQ, Minimum Order |
| **Multi-Unit Franchise** | A franchise arrangement in which a single franchisee operates two or more franchise locations, typically under a Multi-Unit Development Agreement; approximately 54% of all US franchise units are operated by multi-unit franchisees. | Multi-Unit Operator (MUO), Multi-Unit Development |
| **Operations Manual** | The proprietary, confidential document (typically 200–500 pages) specifying every operational procedure in a franchise system — opening/closing procedures, product preparation, service standards, equipment maintenance — that franchisees are contractually required to follow. | Ops Manual, Franchise Operations Manual |
| **Royalty** | The ongoing fee paid by a franchisee to the franchisor as a percentage of gross sales (typically 4–15%), representing the primary ongoing revenue stream for the franchisor and the primary ongoing cost for the franchisee. | Royalty Fee, Franchise Royalty |
| **Same-Store Sales Growth (SSSG)** | The year-over-year percentage change in gross sales for franchise units open for at least 12 months, measuring organic revenue growth independent of new unit openings; the primary indicator of franchise system health. | SSSG, Comparable Store Sales, Comp Sales |
| **Share of Wallet** | The percentage of a customer's total spend in a category captured by a single supplier; the primary metric for measuring account penetration and identifying growth opportunities within existing B2B customer relationships. | Wallet Share, Category Share |
| **Sliding Scale Royalty** | A royalty structure in which the royalty rate varies based on the franchisee's gross sales volume — typically a lower rate for lower-volume units and a higher rate for higher-volume units — designed to balance franchisee economics across a system with wide AUV variation. | Variable Royalty, Tiered Royalty |
| **System-Wide Sales** | The total gross sales revenue generated by all franchise units (company-owned and franchised) within a franchise system in a given period; the primary measure of franchise system scale and the base on which royalties are calculated. | System Sales, Network Sales, Total System Revenue |
| **Technology Fee** | An ongoing fee charged by franchisors for proprietary technology systems (POS, ordering platforms, loyalty programmes) that franchisees are required to use; typically $200–$2,000/month per location, in addition to royalties and advertising fund contributions. | Tech Fee, System Fee |
| **Territory Rights** | The geographic area within which a franchisee has the exclusive or protected right to operate franchise units, defined in the Franchise Agreement; one of the most commercially significant provisions in the franchise relationship. | Franchise Territory, Protected Territory, Exclusive Territory |
| **Tiered Pricing** | A B2B pricing model in which the unit price decreases as order volume increases, structured in defined tiers; incentivises larger orders and rewards high-volume customers with lower unit costs. | Volume Pricing, Quantity Discounts, Price Tiers |
| **Trade Assurance** | Alibaba.com's buyer protection programme that guarantees payment refunds if products do not meet agreed specifications, quality standards, or delivery timelines; reduces the financial risk of cross-border B2B transactions for buyers sourcing from unfamiliar manufacturers. | Buyer Protection, Payment Protection |
| **Trade Credit** | The extension of credit by a supplier to a business customer, allowing the customer to receive goods and pay within a defined period (net-30, net-60, net-90 days); the primary financing mechanism in B2B commerce. | Net Terms, Payment Terms, Supplier Credit |
| **Unit Franchise** | A franchise agreement granting a franchisee the right to operate a single franchise location within a defined territory; the most common franchise structure, representing approximately 60% of all franchise agreements. | Single-Unit Franchise |
| **Vendor-Managed Inventory (VMI)** | A supply chain arrangement in which the supplier (not the customer) is responsible for monitoring and replenishing the customer's inventory, creating deep operational integration and significant switching costs. | VMI, Supplier-Managed Inventory |
| **Wholesaler** | A company that purchases products in bulk from manufacturers and resells them at a markup to retailers or end-users, typically without the full range of services (dedicated sales force, credit, technical support) provided by a full-service distributor. | Wholesale Merchant, Bulk Supplier |
